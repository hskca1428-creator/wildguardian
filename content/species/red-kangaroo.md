# Red Kangaroo (*Osphranter rufus*)

**WildGuardian database profile:** [Species ID 16 — Red Kangaroo](/wildlife/red-kangaroo)

The Red Kangaroo is a large Australian macropod of the arid zone and the largest living marsupial. The accepted scientific name is *Osphranter rufus*; the Atlas of Living Australia lists *Macropus rufus* as a synonym, so that older name may still appear in field guides and records. [1] [2]

> **Safety callout — MEDIUM encounter risk:** A Red Kangaroo is wildlife, not a photo prop. Its size and strength mean an overly close encounter can cause injury. Give it room, do not offer food or water, and let it choose its route away. [3] [4]

## Identification limits: how to recognise a Red Kangaroo

A Red Kangaroo is a very large kangaroo. The Australian Museum gives body lengths of up to 1.4 m for males and up to 1.1 m for females, with a tail up to 1 m. It describes males as tending towards orange-red, females as often blue-grey, and both sexes as paler or whitish underneath. [1]

Look for the combination of a large build, black-and-white cheek patches, and a broad white stripe running from the mouth corner towards the ear. These markings are the Australian Museum’s key features for distinguishing Red Kangaroos from other kangaroo species. [1]

Colour is useful but not conclusive. It can vary between individuals, and the Museum describes it as a tendency rather than a rule. Use the face markings, size, habitat and location together; do not treat a distant sighting or a single photograph as a certain identification. The accepted name for the database profile is *Osphranter rufus*, not the older synonym *Macropus rufus*. [1] [2]

## Australian range and habitat

Red Kangaroos occur across the semi-arid and arid regions of mainland Australia. [1] This is a broad inland distribution statement, not a promise that a kangaroo seen anywhere in that area is a Red Kangaroo; other macropods may share parts of the landscape.

The species uses sparsely wooded or open plains and is recorded in grassland, shrubland, desert, woodland and open forest. It is most common in open savanna woodland, according to the Australian Museum. [1] That mix explains why a sighting can occur in a seemingly open, dry landscape as well as near scattered trees or shrub cover.

For visitors and residents, habitat information is most useful as a cue to look carefully and keep a respectful distance, rather than as a reason to seek a closer view. In parks, NSW National Parks and Wildlife Service asks visitors to watch native animals from a distance and stay on tracks to reduce disturbance. [4]

## Behaviour and ecology

Red Kangaroos are herbivores. Their diet is primarily grasses, with forbs and the leaves of shrubs also eaten. [1] The Australian Museum notes that the water in this herbage and foliage can meet water as well as nutritional needs when water is scarce; during drought, reduced food supply can affect Red Kangaroo populations. [1]

Reproduction is sensitive to environmental conditions. Red Kangaroos can breed throughout the year, although the Museum says most young are born in spring and summer. Females have one young at a time and can delay birth until an older joey has left the pouch, a process called embryonic diapause. [1]

A sighting should not be read as a fixed behavioural script. Queensland’s wildlife guidance stresses that kangaroos and wallabies are individuals that learn from experience. It also notes that kangaroos are active during the day but mostly feed at dawn and dusk. Treat that as a sensible time to be especially alert around known kangaroo areas, not as a guarantee of when every Red Kangaroo will be present. [3]

## Safer encounters with Red Kangaroos

Keeping space is the central safety measure. Queensland advises giving kangaroos as much room as possible and moving away if one comes towards you, even when it seems to be seeking food or human contact. It also advises against approaching animals that are mating, sparring, growling or clucking, and against moving between a female and her joey. [3]

Use these practical steps if you encounter a Red Kangaroo:

- **Watch from a distance and do not feed it.** Queensland warns that food or water can make kangaroos dependent on people and increase the chance of aggressive behaviour; NSW National Parks likewise says feeding can make native animals sick, demanding and aggressive. [3] [4]
- **Do not shoo, corner or try to make it move.** Gestures or objects can frighten an animal and make it feel it must defend itself. [3]
- **Choose space over a closer photograph.** Use a camera zoom rather than closing the gap. [4]
- **If it appears threatened or approaches, move slowly towards a safe place.** Queensland advises a short, deep cough, avoiding eye contact, keeping arms close, and not turning your back or running. Put a physical barrier such as a tree or fence between you and the animal where possible. [3]
- **If an attack occurs, protect yourself rather than fighting back.** Queensland advises dropping to the ground, curling into a ball if able or lying face down, and using your arms to protect your head and neck until the animal moves away. [3]

On roads, be particularly alert where wildlife-crossing signs are present and in places with evidence of wildlife road deaths. Queensland advises slowing in those places, particularly at night; NSW National Parks also advises avoiding driving at dawn and dusk and scanning the road and verges ahead. [3] [4]

## Injury or medical response

An emergency comes first. If someone has been seriously injured, call **Triple Zero (000)** immediately. Healthdirect says the call taker can give situation-specific first-aid advice while help is on the way. [5]

Do not create a second casualty by going back towards the kangaroo. Healthdirect’s DRSABCD approach begins with checking for danger to yourself, bystanders and the injured person. If the person is responsive, its guidance is to make them comfortable, monitor breathing, and manage severe bleeding; if there is no response, send for help immediately. [5]

For a sick or injured kangaroo, keep clear rather than approaching it. Queensland warns that sick or injured kangaroos can become defensive and says to contact a trained wildlife rehabilitator instead. NSW National Parks directs people who find sick, injured or orphaned wildlife to a licensed wildlife rescue and rehabilitation group, local vet or the nearest National Parks office. [3] [4]

For non-emergency health guidance in Australia, Healthdirect can be called on **1800 022 222**. [5]

## Frequently asked questions

### Is *Osphranter rufus* the correct scientific name for the Red Kangaroo?

Yes. The Atlas of Living Australia lists *Osphranter rufus* as the accepted name, with the Australian Faunal Directory as name authority. It lists *Macropus rufus* as a synonym. [2]

### Where in Australia do Red Kangaroos live?

They are found through the semi-arid and arid regions of mainland Australia, in habitats that include open plains, grassland, shrubland, desert, woodland and open forest. [1]

### Can I identify a Red Kangaroo just by red fur?

No. Males tend to be orange-red and females are often blue-grey, so colour alone is not a reliable test. Look for the black-and-white cheek patches and broad white facial stripe as well, and retain uncertainty when the view is poor. [1]

### What should I do if a kangaroo comes towards me?

Give it space and move away slowly to safety. Do not run, stare, wave your arms, offer food or act aggressively. Queensland recommends making yourself less threatening and placing a barrier between yourself and the kangaroo where possible. [3]

### Should I approach an injured Red Kangaroo?

No. An injured kangaroo may be defensive. Keep clear and contact an appropriately trained wildlife rescue service, a local vet or the relevant parks authority rather than approaching the animal. [3] [4]
