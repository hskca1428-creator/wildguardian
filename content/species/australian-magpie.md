# Australian Magpie (*Gymnorhina tibicen*)

The Australian Magpie is a familiar black-and-white songbird of open, tree-lined places in Australia. Its loud carolling and confident ground-foraging make it easy to notice, while its seasonal nest defence can make a small number of encounters feel confronting. [2] [3]

**WildGuardian database profile:** [Australian Magpie — ID 13](/wildlife/australian-magpie) · **Risk label:** MEDIUM

> **Safety callout:** A swooping Australian Magpie is usually defending a nest or young, rather than seeking people out. Stay calm, protect your face and eyes, and move carefully out of the area. Choose another route while swooping continues. [3] [4]

## Scientific name and database record

The accepted scientific name is ***Gymnorhina tibicen***. The Atlas of Living Australia, using the Australian Faunal Directory as its source, lists that name as accepted and lists ***Cracticus tibicen*** as a synonym. The supplied database name is therefore current and has been retained for this page and its linkable ID 13 profile. [1]

The species belongs to the family Artamidae. Australian Museum and NSW Government species accounts also use *Gymnorhina tibicen* for the Australian Magpie. [2] [3]

## Identification limits

An Australian Magpie is typically recognised by a black head, belly and tail tip, with white on the wings, lower back, tail and rear of the head. Its bill is blue-grey, its legs black and its eyes brown. [3]

Those features are a useful starting point, not a guarantee from a quick glimpse. Plumage pattern varies across the species’ range; for example, the Australian Museum notes geographic variation in the amount and placement of white, as well as differences between males and females. [2] A clear view of the overall black-and-white pattern, bill and behaviour is more useful than relying on one patch of colour.

The bird is often seen walking across grass as it searches the ground. That behaviour can support an identification, but it is not enough on its own. The Australian Museum lists Pied Butcherbird and Magpie-lark as similar species, so uncertainty is a good reason to keep a respectful distance and avoid treating a bird as a problem based only on a brief sighting. [2]

## Australian range and habitat

The Australian Faunal Directory’s family account places *Gymnorhina tibicen* in Australia and New Guinea. [7] Within Australia, the Australian Museum describes Australian Magpies as occurring where trees sit beside open areas, including parks and playing fields. It says they are absent from the densest forests and arid deserts. [2]

That habitat pattern explains why encounters commonly happen near suburban lawns, sports grounds, school grounds, streetscapes and rural clearings with mature trees. It does **not** mean every open park holds a territorial pair, or that a bird seen in one location will stay there indefinitely. A territory needs places to feed, roost and nest, and magpies actively defend the territories on which those needs depend. [2]

## Behaviour and ecology

Australian Magpies commonly forage on the ground for insects and their larvae. NSW Government guidance likewise describes them feeding on small insects and animals at or just below the ground surface. [2] [3]

Their well-known musical call is called **carolling**. Pairs or groups may carol to announce or defend a territory, and the Australian Museum describes both duet and group performances. [2] This territorial behaviour is an important part of their normal ecology, rather than evidence that every magpie near people is aggressive.

During the breeding period, some individual birds defend nest areas when people enter too closely. The Australian Museum identifies August to November as the peak breeding season, while Queensland guidance describes the broad swooping season as spring, from August to November. NSW guidance notes that nest defence by an individual bird commonly lasts about four to six weeks. Exact timing and the places where a particular bird defends will vary locally. [2] [3] [4]

Most Australian Magpies do not swoop people. When a defensive bird does swoop, it is often a low, fast warning flight; contact is occasional rather than inevitable. [2] [3] The practical concern is not only direct contact but also the chance of losing concentration near roads, paths or while cycling.

## Safer encounters with a swooping magpie

The safest response is to leave the defended area without escalating the encounter. Queensland and NSW agencies recommend practical avoidance and protective measures rather than confrontation. [3] [4]

- **Move calmly and carefully away**, then use an alternative route while the bird is swooping. Looking towards the bird while leaving can reduce the chance of a swoop; keep your attention on the path and traffic as well. [3] [4]
- **Protect your head and eyes** with a broad-brimmed hat, sunglasses, a bicycle helmet or an open umbrella. These measures are recommended to reduce the chance of face and eye injury. [4] [5]
- **If cycling, dismount and walk the bicycle through the area.** Both NSW and Queensland guidance support getting off the bike rather than continuing to ride through a known defence zone. [3] [4]
- **Do not fight back, throw objects or swing at the bird.** Queensland warns that this can make future responses more aggressive. Do not approach a young magpie; a parent may be nearby and defensive. [4]

It is reasonable to let other people know about a known swooping location through appropriate local reporting or warning signs, especially where children or cyclists use the area. NSW advises reporting a bird considered a serious menace to the relevant local authority. Requirements and response arrangements differ by state and council. [3] [4]

## Injury or medical response

Direct strikes are uncommon, but eye injuries from magpie encounters can be serious. The Lions Eye Institute reports injuries including corneal abrasions, penetrating injuries and infections; severe cases can cause permanent vision loss. [5]

If a swoop affects an eye, seek urgent medical assessment for worsening pain, any change or loss of vision, blurred vision, black spots, bleeding, discharge, or difficulty opening the eye. Healthdirect gives this escalation advice for eye trauma. [6]

If something has pierced the eye, go to the nearest emergency department or call **Triple Zero (000)** for an ambulance. For non-emergency health advice in Australia, Healthdirect’s 24-hour helpline is **1800 022 222**. [6] If a swoop causes a fall, treat the fall or head injury as the immediate concern and seek appropriate medical help.

## FAQs

### Is *Gymnorhina tibicen* still the accepted scientific name?

Yes. The Atlas of Living Australia lists *Gymnorhina tibicen* as the accepted name sourced from the Australian Faunal Directory. It records *Cracticus tibicen* as a synonym. [1]

### Why do Australian Magpies swoop?

Swooping is a territorial response linked to protecting eggs and young during the breeding season. It is generally temporary and centred on a defended nest area, not a sign that all Australian Magpies are dangerous. [3] [4]

### Do all Australian Magpies swoop people?

No. The Australian Museum says only a small minority behave this way. A bird may also defend only during breeding, or react to particular people entering a particular territory. [2]

### What should I do if a magpie swoops while I am cycling?

Get off the bicycle and walk it carefully through the area. Keep your helmet on, protect your face and eyes, and take an alternative route on future trips while the defence behaviour continues. [3] [4]

### When should I get medical help after a magpie encounter?

Get urgent assessment for an eye injury with worsening pain, vision change, blur, black spots, blood or discharge. A penetrating eye injury needs emergency department care or an ambulance via 000. [5] [6]
