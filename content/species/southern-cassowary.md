# Southern Cassowary (*Casuarius casuarius johnsonii*)

The southern cassowary is a large, flightless rainforest bird of northern Queensland. It is an important seed disperser, but it is also a powerful wild animal that can be dangerous if cornered. The most useful response to a sighting is calm observation from well back, without food, pursuit or contact. [1] [2]

**Taxonomy note and database continuity.** WildGuardian’s supplied **Cassowary** record is at species level as *Casuarius casuarius* (ID 17). For the Australian animal covered here, Queensland and Commonwealth species profiles use ***Casuarius casuarius johnsonii***—the Australian southern cassowary subspecies. This page therefore uses that more precise current Australian name while retaining the [Cassowary database profile — *Casuarius casuarius* (ID 17)](/wildlife/southern-cassowary). [1] [3]

> **Safety callout:** Do not approach or feed a cassowary, and never go between chicks and their father. Queensland’s guidance says a person injured by a cassowary should call **000 immediately**. [2]

## Identification limits: recognising a cassowary without closing the distance

An adult southern cassowary has coarse black, hair-like body feathers, a tall greyish casque or “helmet”, a bright blue neck and long red wattles. It has strong legs with three toes and an enlarged, dagger-shaped inner claw. Queensland describes adults as reaching up to 2 metres tall; females can weigh up to 76 kg and males up to 55 kg. [1]

Young birds look different. Queensland notes that juveniles are browner, with duller head and neck colours, while chicks are striped yellow and black. [1] The Australian Museum also records that females are generally larger and brighter than males, but these features are not a safe way to make a close-range judgement about an individual bird. [4] [2]

The clear colours and casque can make the species easy to recognise, yet identification is not an invitation to get a better photo. Cassowaries are wild, can be unpredictable and may feel threatened when people approach. A bird near a road, campsite, garden or fruit tree should be treated as a safety situation, not a viewing opportunity. [2]

## Australian range and habitat

In Australia, southern cassowaries occur only in **Queensland**. Queensland’s environment department describes three remaining populations: one in the Wet Tropics and two on Cape York. The Wet Tropics population extends from Cooktown to the Paluma Range; on Cape York, the southern population occurs in the vine forests of the McIlwraith and Iron ranges, and a northern population occurs in vine forests north of Shelburne Bay. [1]

The Commonwealth describes the Australian distribution more broadly as the Wet Tropics and Cape York Peninsula, and says that historical forest clearance has greatly reduced and fragmented occurrence within the species’ natural range. [3] In the Wet Tropics, most remaining essential habitat is on protected tenures, but protection does not remove the need for care around roads, homes and other places where people and cassowaries overlap. [1]

Dense tropical rainforest and woodland with many fruiting trees are core habitat. [1] The birds can be difficult to see in this vegetation because they are secretive and solitary; rumbling calls and grunts may be heard before a bird is seen. [1] [4] Do not leave a path, track or vehicle to investigate a call. [2]

## Ecology: a rainforest seed carrier

Southern cassowaries mainly eat fruit that has fallen to the ground. Their diet can also include fungi, snails, insects, plants and small dead animals. Queensland has recorded more than 238 plant species in the diet. [1] Fruit eaten by a cassowary should never be assumed safe for people: the Australian Museum notes that the bird can digest some foods poisonous to humans. [4]

The species is often called a rainforest gardener because its droppings distribute seeds. Queensland states that some large seeds can be swallowed and dispersed by no other animal, and that the bird’s digestive process can help some seeds germinate. [1] The Commonwealth likewise identifies seed dispersal as an important contribution to rainforest plant diversity and community structure. [3]

Adults generally live alone. Contact is mainly associated with breeding, and a female may mate with more than one male in a season. The male incubates the eggs for about 50 days and guards the chicks afterwards. [1] That family arrangement matters for safety: Queensland specifically warns people never to approach chicks or place themselves between chicks and their father, because males defend their young. [2]

## Conservation context in Queensland

The Australian southern cassowary is listed as **Endangered** under the Commonwealth *Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999*. [3] Queensland also lists the species as Endangered, and identifies habitat loss, fragmentation and modification, vehicle strikes, introduced predators including dogs and pigs, domestic-dog attacks, and climate-driven extreme weather as threats. [1]

WildGuardian’s supplied record carries a **MEDIUM** risk label. To keep terms distinct, this page reports that label separately from the official national conservation status, which is Endangered. [1] [3]

For people living in or visiting cassowary country, prevention helps both the bird and the community. Queensland says feeding is illegal, dangerous and has resulted in cassowary deaths. Food supplied deliberately or left accessible can make a bird bolder around people; roadside feeding can condition it to associate cars with food and increase vehicle-strike risk. [1] [2]

## Safer encounters in cassowary country

Use Queensland’s official advice rather than trying to manage, move or test a cassowary yourself:

- **Do not approach, feed or chase the bird.** Keep food, cooking and cleaning items secured, place scraps in closed bins, and do not try to photograph from close range. [2]
- **If a cassowary approaches, remain calm, face it and slowly back away.** Do not turn your back. Put a tree, backpack or another available object between you and the bird where practical; keep food, drink and phones out of sight because cassowaries may mistake them for food. [2]
- **Be especially cautious around chicks.** Stay away and do not position yourself between chicks and their father. [2]
- **On roads, slow down in cassowary habitat and never stop in traffic to watch.** Safely avoid the bird and be alert for young following an adult. [2]
- **Keep dogs contained at home and on a leash when walking.** Queensland warns that dog–cassowary interactions can injure or kill either animal. [2]

These steps are deliberately about separation and avoidance. Do not attempt to handle, capture, feed or relocate a cassowary.

## Injury, sick birds and urgent response

A cassowary’s kick can break bones, and its claw is capable of serious injury. [1] Queensland’s unambiguous direction is to call **000 immediately** if a person has been injured by a cassowary. [2] This page does not substitute for emergency assessment or give animal-handling directions.

For a sick, injured or orphaned cassowary, Queensland advises reporting it to **1300 130 372** (option 1, then option 2), through the QWildlife app, or via the online sighting form. Do not approach or try to move the bird. [2] A routine sighting can also be logged through QWildlife or the online form, which contributes information for conservation management. [1] [2]

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the southern cassowary endangered in Australia?

Yes. The Australian southern cassowary is listed as Endangered under the Commonwealth EPBC Act. [3] Queensland also identifies habitat fragmentation, vehicle strikes, dogs, pigs and severe weather among the pressures it faces. [1]

### Where do southern cassowaries live in Australia?

They live only in northern Queensland: the Wet Tropics from Cooktown to the Paluma Range, plus two remaining Cape York populations in vine forests. [1]

### Are cassowaries aggressive?

They should be treated as potentially dangerous rather than as tame wildlife. Queensland says they can be aggressive and unpredictable, particularly if they feel threatened; the most protective course is to give them space and never feed them. [2]

### Can I feed a cassowary if it comes near a campsite or road?

No. Queensland says feeding cassowaries is illegal and dangerous. Secure food and rubbish instead; feeding can make birds bold around people and roads. [2]

### What should I do if a cassowary or its chicks are on the track ahead?

Do not approach or move between a father and chicks. Remain calm, face the bird and slowly back away, maintaining separation rather than trying to pass closely. [2]
