# Eastern Grey Kangaroo (*Macropus giganteus*)

The **Eastern Grey Kangaroo** is a large Australian macropod marsupial. The supplied scientific name, *Macropus giganteus*, remains current for this profile: the Australian Faunal Directory-backed Atlas of Living Australia lists it as the **accepted** name. [1] WildGuardian’s **MEDIUM** label is an encounter-risk prompt, not a conservation status or a substitute for local wildlife advice.

## Identification limits: what an Eastern Grey Kangaroo looks like

Australian Museum information describes the Eastern Grey Kangaroo as light-grey and woolly-coated, with a darker face and a dark tip to the tail. Its hind legs and feet are much larger than its forelimbs. The long, muscular tail helps with balance while hopping and can support the animal during slow movement. [2]

Size can be impressive, particularly in males. The Australian Museum gives body lengths to 1.3 metres and tails to 1 metre for males; females are smaller in the figures it provides. [2] Size alone is not a safe way to identify an animal or to judge its behaviour. It is better to keep your distance than to move closer for a clearer look.

Identification has real limits where grey kangaroos share country. The NSW Scientific Committee records that *M. giganteus* occurs alongside its sister grey-kangaroo species in parts of south-west Queensland, north-west Victoria and western NSW. [3] In those overlap areas, do not treat a single coat colour, a distant view or a large body as enough for a high-confidence field identification. If identification is needed for a formal report or management question, seek guidance from the relevant wildlife authority or qualified expert rather than approaching the animal. [3]

## Australian range and habitat

Eastern Grey Kangaroos occur across much of eastern Australia, including Tasmania. The Australian Museum describes a broad, almost continuous distribution between inland plains and the coast in areas receiving more than 250 millimetres of annual rainfall. [2] The NSW Scientific Committee’s account similarly describes a distribution from Cape York Peninsula through eastern Australia to far south-eastern South Australia and north-eastern Tasmania. [3] These descriptions are useful at a national scale, but they do not guarantee that the species occurs at any particular reserve, suburb or property.

Their habitat is varied. They use semi-arid mallee scrub, woodlands, forests and some farmland that retains native vegetation, and may favour denser scrub and forest for cover. [2] Eastern Grey Kangaroos can also use altered landscapes that still offer grass, shelter and water, including golf courses, outer-suburban parks, rural residential areas and farmland. [4] That adaptability helps explain why people may see them near the bushland edge as well as in more rural settings.

## Behaviour and ecology

This species is primarily a grazer. It is herbivorous, with grasses a major part of its diet; the Australian Museum notes a preference for young, green grass shoots and reports that other plants and sometimes fungi may also be eaten. [2] The NSW Scientific Committee likewise describes it as a specialist grazer whose diet is mainly grasses, with some other plant material consumed. [3]

Eastern Grey Kangaroos are largely crepuscular or nocturnal. They commonly rest in shade or dense vegetation through the day, then move to more open, grassy places to feed from late afternoon into the night. [2] [3] They are social animals. The Australian Museum notes mobs of 10 or more, while the NSW Scientific Committee describes fluid groups that can form part of larger social units. [2] [3]

A close animal is not necessarily a tame animal. Queensland guidance notes that repeated feeding can teach kangaroos to approach people for food and that, when food is not provided, animals may become aggressive. [4] Keep observation passive and give the animal room to choose where it goes.

> **Safety callout — give kangaroos space.** Eastern Grey Kangaroos are large, powerful wild animals capable of injuring people. Risk is higher when animals are accustomed to being fed, when people get close to fighting or courting kangaroos, or when a female is protecting a young animal. [4] [5]

## Safer encounters with Eastern Grey Kangaroos

A sighting is usually best treated as wildlife viewing, not an invitation to interact. Queensland and NSW National Parks guidance both stress distance and avoiding food rewards. [4] [5]

- **Do not feed or provide water.** It can habituate kangaroos to people, create dependence and increase the chance of aggressive behaviour. [4]
- **Stay well back and do not try to shoo an animal away.** Avoid walking directly at it, gesturing with objects or forcing it to move. [4]
- **Give extra space to a mob, a female with a young animal, and animals that are sparring, fighting, courting, growling or clucking.** These situations are specifically identified in Queensland and NSW safety guidance as higher-risk contexts. [4] [5]
- **If walking with a dog where dogs are permitted, keep it on a lead.** Kangaroos can feel threatened by dogs and may defend themselves. [5]
- **Drive attentively where kangaroos may cross, especially around dawn, dusk and at night.** Slow down for wildlife signs and areas where animals are known to cross. [4] [5]

If a kangaroo moves toward you or you feel threatened, do not run or turn your back. Move away slowly toward a safe place, keep your movements calm, and place a solid barrier such as a tree or fence between you and the animal where possible. Do not strike it. NSW National Parks also advises staying low and protecting the face and back of the neck if an approaching kangaroo continues to close the distance. [4] [5]

## If someone is injured

Get to safety first and seek urgent help for a serious injury. Healthdirect Australia advises that **any animal bite which breaks the skin should be assessed by a doctor as soon as possible**. [6] Go to the nearest emergency department for a large or deep wound, a wound to the face or head, or bleeding that cannot be stopped; call Triple Zero (000) for an ambulance if urgent medical help is needed. [6]

For a sick, injured or orphaned kangaroo, keep back. Queensland guidance warns that sick or injured kangaroos may be defensive when approached and directs people to contact a trained wildlife rehabilitator instead. [4] In NSW national parks, the direction is to contact a licensed wildlife rescue and rehabilitation group, local vet or the nearest parks office. [5] Do not attempt to handle, feed, capture or move the animal yourself.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is *Macropus giganteus* still the accepted scientific name?

Yes. The Atlas of Living Australia, using the Australian Faunal Directory as its name authority, lists *Macropus giganteus* Shaw, 1790 as the accepted name for the Eastern Grey Kangaroo. [1]

### Where in Australia are Eastern Grey Kangaroos found?

They have a broad eastern-Australian distribution that includes Tasmania. They occur in a range of habitats from mallee and woodland to forest and some modified landscapes with suitable grass, water and shelter. [2] [4]

### Are Eastern Grey Kangaroos active at night?

They are largely crepuscular or nocturnal. They often shelter during the day and feed in more open areas from late afternoon through the night. [2] [3]

### Why should I never feed a kangaroo?

Government guidance says feeding or watering can make kangaroos dependent on people and more likely to approach expecting food, which can increase aggressive encounters. [4]

### Does WildGuardian’s MEDIUM label mean the species is threatened?

No. It is an encounter-safety label for this WildGuardian profile. The Australian Museum lists the species as IUCN Least Concern; conservation assessments and wildlife rules should always be checked with the relevant authority for the place and purpose involved. [2]
