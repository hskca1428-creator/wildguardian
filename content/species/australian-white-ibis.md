# Australian White Ibis (*Threskiornis molucca*)

The Australian White Ibis is a large native waterbird that many Australians know from wetlands, playing fields, parks and rubbish bins. It is an ibis in the family Threskiornithidae, with a mostly white body, bare black head and neck, black legs and a long, down-curved black bill. Australian Museum gives its length as 69–76 centimetres. [2] The species is sometimes called “bin chicken”, a nickname that reflects urban scavenging rather than its full ecological story. Its usual food includes terrestrial and aquatic invertebrates, and it is a natural part of wetland and grassland systems. [2] [3]

> **WildGuardian record label:** LOW. This is the supplied record label, not a statement of legal protection or a conservation assessment.

## Name and identification limits

This database record uses *Threskiornis molucca*, a spelling also used by the Australian Museum and NSW environment material. [2] [4] There is a taxonomic naming wrinkle worth knowing: the Atlas of Living Australia, citing the Australian Faunal Directory as its name authority, accepts *Threskiornis moluccus* for the Australian White Ibis. [1] Both spellings occur in Australian authoritative sources for this bird; they should not be treated as two different ibis species.

An adult’s white plumage and bare black head and neck are strong field marks. The black-tipped flight feathers are visible when it flies, and groups may fly in a V formation. [2] [3] During breeding, the small skin patch beneath the wing can change from dull pink to dark scarlet; adults also have cream plumes at the base of the neck. [2] Young birds can look different because black feathers cover the neck. [2] [3]

These details help with an informed observation, but they are not a substitute for a careful identification where a record, rescue or management decision depends on it. The name “Sacred Ibis” can add confusion: the Australian Museum notes that it more properly refers to a closely related African species. [2] A bird seen briefly, at distance or in poor light should be left as an uncertain ibis rather than assigned a species from colour alone.

## Australian range and habitat

Australian government and museum sources consistently describe the core range as northern, eastern and south-western Australia. [2] [3] In its natural settings, the bird uses swamps, lagoons, floodplains, freshwater wetlands and grasslands. [2] [3] NSW’s wetland agency includes ibis among waterbirds that use swamps, lagoons, mudflats, estuaries, lakes, rivers, floodplain wetlands and dams. [4]

The bird is not confined to water’s edge. Australian Museum says it can be observed in all but the driest habitats and has become a successful resident of urban parks and gardens. [2] Victorian government material similarly describes it as widespread across northern, eastern and south-western Australia and as a resident of parks and gardens in urban centres. [3]

One distribution detail deserves restraint. The Australian Museum says the species is absent from Tasmania, while the Museums Victoria species account lists Tasmania in its distribution. [2] [7] Rather than present a categorical claim where these authoritative pages disagree, this guide limits its range statement to their shared mainland pattern. A local sighting can be checked through an appropriate regional biodiversity record service.

## Ecology: a wetland bird that also uses cities

The down-curved bill is a working tool. It probes mud and soil for food, and the bird eats terrestrial and aquatic invertebrates; Australian Museum specifically lists crayfish and mussels among favoured foods. [2] In NSW wetlands, ibis are classed as invertebrate feeders on mudflats and shallow edges, while large waders including ibis feed in shallow floodplain habitats. [4] Victorian guidance also notes that probing for insects can aerate soil on parks and sporting ovals. [3]

Australian White Ibis can move long distances to places with better food availability. [3] They breed in large colonies, sometimes alongside Straw-necked Ibis, and one or two broods may be raised in a year. [2] More broadly, NSW describes ibises as colonial nesting waterbirds whose major breeding events in floodplain wetlands need substantial floods. [4]

City living is not the whole story of the species. Some urban populations use food scraps, landfill and litter, while the Australian Museum cautions that abundance is decreasing in the natural range even though ibis have become more common in some places. [2] [3] Seeing many ibis in a city therefore does not, by itself, describe the condition of wetlands or the species everywhere.

## Safer, respectful encounters

An ibis looking for food in a park is best observed with space around it. Sutherland Shire Council says birds may take food from picnickers but are not considered dangerous or aggressive, even though their size and bill can feel intimidating at close quarters. [5] Keep children and pets from crowding birds, particularly around a food source or a group of birds, and allow the ibis a clear way to move away.

Food scraps and unsecured rubbish attract ibis to urban places. Victorian and NSW local-government guidance both identify accessible artificial food as a driver of urban problems; keeping litter contained and bins closed reduces that access. [3] [5] Avoid offering food to wildlife. This supports a cleaner shared space without teaching birds to approach people for a meal. [5]

The bird is protected wildlife in NSW under the *Biodiversity Conservation Act 2016*, and the council says it is unlawful there to harm ibis or collect their eggs. [5] In Victoria, ibis and other native wildlife are protected under the *Wildlife Act 1975*, with penalties for harming or destroying protected wildlife. [3] These are jurisdiction-specific examples, not a substitute for checking rules where you are. If an ibis is injured, Sutherland Shire Council directs people to an authorised wildlife rescue contact rather than personal intervention. [5]

## Medical response after an injury

No ibis-specific medical protocol is stated here, and bat-exposure advice does **not** apply to an ibis encounter. If an ibis bite breaks the skin, Healthdirect's general advice for animal bites is to see a doctor as soon as possible. For a large or deep wound, uncontrolled bleeding, or an injury to the face or head, Healthdirect advises urgent emergency assessment; call triple zero (000) for urgent help. [6]

## Frequently asked questions

### Is an Australian White Ibis the same as a “bin chicken”?

“Bin chicken” is an informal nickname for the Australian White Ibis. It arose because some urban birds use rubbish, bins and landfill food, but the species is also a native wetland and grassland bird that feeds on invertebrates. [2] [3]

### How can I recognise a young Australian White Ibis?

Young birds resemble adults in overall shape, but their neck is covered with black feathers. Adults have a bare black head and neck, plus cream neck-base plumes in breeding condition. [2]

### Does the Australian White Ibis live only in wetlands?

No. Wetlands, floodplains, swamps, lagoons and grasslands are important habitat, yet the species also occurs in parks and gardens in Australian urban areas. [2] [3]

### Are Australian White Ibis protected?

They are protected in NSW and Victoria under the state laws identified above. [3] [5] Protection and wildlife-management arrangements can differ between jurisdictions, so use the relevant state or territory authority for a local question.

### Are ibis aggressive around food?

Sutherland Shire Council reports that ibis may steal food from picnickers but are not dangerous or aggressive. Their size can still be confronting, so keeping space between people, pets and birds is the more respectful response. [5]
