# Mouse Spider (*Missulena* spp.)

**WildGuardian risk label: HIGH.** A mouse spider is not one precisely identifiable species from a distance. It is the common name used for spiders in the Australian genus *Missulena*. They are striking, ground-dwelling mygalomorph spiders that can resemble funnel-web spiders, and a suspected bite needs emergency treatment. [2] [3]

> **Safety callout — do not touch or attempt to handle a suspected mouse spider.** A bite from a mouse spider, or from an unidentified big black spider, should be treated as a medical emergency in Australia: call Triple Zero (000) for an ambulance. [3] [4]

## Scientific name and database profile

The supplied database wording, **“Missulena species”**, is a genus-level label rather than a complete scientific species name. The Atlas of Living Australia, using the Australian Faunal Directory as name authority, lists ***Missulena* Walckenaer, 1805** as the accepted genus in the family Actinopodidae. [1]

This profile therefore uses ***Missulena* spp.**: *spp.* signals that the page covers more than one species in the genus. It does **not** assign every Australian mouse spider sighting to a single species. The linkable WildGuardian database profile remains **ID 11** at `/species/mouse-spider/`. This distinction matters because colour, size, burrow form, activity and local distribution can differ between species.

## Identification limits: what a mouse spider can look like

Australian Museum guidance describes mouse spiders as stout, generally black spiders with distinctly bulbous head and jaw regions. Their body length ranges from about 10 to 35 mm. Depending on the species, the abdomen may be black, dark blue, or black with a pale patch; some males have red or orange-red jaws and head area. [2]

Features used by specialists include a smooth, shiny carapace; eyes spread across the front of the carapace rather than tightly clustered; and short spinnerets with a domed, button-like final segment. Females are generally larger and more solidly built, while males can have longer, more slender legs and conspicuous colour markings. [2]

Those features are useful context, not a safe field test. Mouse spiders are often confused with funnel-web spiders, and an individual may not show the colour or sex traits people expect. [2] Do not rely on a red head, a black body, a burrow, or a photograph alone to decide whether a spider is harmless. Treat uncertainty around a large dark ground spider conservatively and keep hands away.

## Australian range and habitat

Mouse spiders occur across **much of mainland Australia**. The Australian Museum records the group in habitats from open forest to semi-arid shrubland. [2] This is a broad genus-level statement, not a promise that every *Missulena* species occurs throughout that area. A page about a mouse spider should not turn a continent-scale group range into a species-level local record.

They are ground dwellers associated with burrows, and can sometimes turn up in suburban gardens. At the same time, the Australian Museum says mouse spiders are not usually abundant in heavily populated urban areas. [2] Burrows should not be used as a certain identification clue. The museum notes that burrow construction varies and that comparatively little is known about the burrows of some mouse-spider species. [2]

For that reason, a small ground opening, silk, or a spider seen near a garden does not confirm this genus. It is enough reason to avoid putting bare hands into the opening or around it.

## Behaviour and ecological role

Mouse spiders are predators. Insects are their main prey, and the Australian Museum reports that prey is commonly ambushed from the safety of a burrow; mouse spiders have also been observed foraging outside at night. [2] As with other spiders, this predatory role is part of the local invertebrate food web, not a reason to approach one.

Adult males are the mouse spiders people are more likely to encounter away from a burrow. The museum says males often wander in search of females from late summer to about April or May. [2] That timing is a broad observation for the group, not a calendar that can identify a spider in a particular suburb. Females are often more closely associated with burrows, and the behaviour of less-studied species should not be assumed from one well-known member of the genus. [2]

The safest interpretation of an unexpected mouse-spider-like animal is simple: give it space. A spider may bite when it is frightened or threatened, and government guidance advises people not to touch spiders. [5]

## Safer encounters around homes, gardens and bushland

Avoid turning an encounter into an identification exercise. Do not pick up a spider, probe a suspected burrow with your fingers, or encourage children or pets to approach it. Victorian health guidance specifically advises teaching children not to touch spiders and using care around gardens and footwear. [5]

Practical precautions are limited but worthwhile:

- Keep bare hands out of ground holes, leaf litter and other places where a large ground spider may be concealed.
- Check shoes, gloves and clothing that have been left on the ground before putting them on. [5]
- Give the animal room and use a cautious route around it rather than attempting to move it yourself.

These are encounter-avoidance measures, not a request to remove or manage the spider. If a bite may have occurred, medical care takes priority over identifying the animal.

## Suspected bite: emergency response in Australia

Mouse-spider bites are uncommon, but Healthdirect says their symptoms can resemble those of a funnel-web spider bite. Reported concerning signs include intense pain at the bite site, sweating, nausea, vomiting or abdominal pain; salivation, muscle twitching, breathing difficulty, confusion and numbness around the mouth or tongue can also occur. [3] Symptoms are not a test for the spider’s identity. **Do not wait to see whether they develop.**

Healthdirect’s instruction is clear: if someone has been bitten by a big black spider, call Triple Zero (000) and ask for an ambulance. [3] St John Ambulance Australia gives the same mouse-spider-specific emergency direction. [4]

While waiting for the ambulance, follow these official first-aid steps for a suspected mouse-spider bite:

1. **Keep the person lying down and as still as possible.** [3] [4]
2. **If the bite is on a limb, apply pressure immobilisation.** Put an elasticated roller bandage over the bite, then bandage the whole limb from above the fingers or toes towards the body. The bandage should be firm but must not cut off circulation; immobilise the limb with a splint. [3] [4]
3. **Leave the bandage in place and wait for emergency care.** Do not cut the wound or apply a tourniquet. [2] [5]

Pressure immobilisation is specifically recommended for suspected funnel-web or mouse-spider bites, not for ordinary spider bites. [3] Do not attempt to handle, capture or otherwise pursue the spider after a bite. The emergency call and first aid should not be delayed for identification.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a mouse spider the same as a funnel-web spider?

No. They are different spiders, but the Australian Museum notes that mouse spiders are often confused with funnel-web spiders. [2] That overlap in appearance is why a suspected bite from a mouse spider or an unidentified big black spider is treated urgently. [3]

### What is the correct scientific name for a mouse spider?

For this broad database record, the correct form is ***Missulena* spp.** The accepted taxonomic name is the genus ***Missulena***, not a single species called “*Missulena species*”. The Atlas of Living Australia identifies *Missulena* as an accepted genus with the Australian Faunal Directory as its name authority. [1]

### Where are mouse spiders found in Australia?

At genus level, they are found over much of mainland Australia, from open forest to semi-arid shrubland. [2] Individual species do not necessarily share the same range, so a precise local or state distribution needs a species-level identification and appropriate records.

### Does a red head prove that a spider is a mouse spider?

No. Some male mouse spiders have red or orange-red jaws and head regions, but other mouse spiders are black overall and other large spiders may also be dark or variably coloured. [2] Never use colour alone to decide it is safe to touch a spider.

### What should I do if I think a mouse spider has bitten someone?

Call Triple Zero (000) for an ambulance, keep the person still, and use pressure immobilisation if the bite is on a limb. [3] [4] Treat the situation as urgent even if the spider cannot be confidently identified.
