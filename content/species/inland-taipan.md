# Inland Taipan (*Oxyuranus microlepidotus*)

The **Inland Taipan** is an Australian elapid snake also known as the Fierce Snake, Small-scaled Snake and Lignum Snake. Its scientific name is *Oxyuranus microlepidotus*. It is a remote-country species associated with the Channel Country and its cracking-clay landscapes, rather than a snake most Australians are likely to encounter. [1]

> **Safety first:** Treat a suspected snake bite as an emergency. **Call Triple Zero (000) for an ambulance and follow official advice; do not wait to identify the snake.** All snake bites should be treated as potentially life-threatening, even when the bite seems minor or the snake was not clearly seen. [3] [4]

## Identification: useful features, firm limits

An Inland Taipan is a medium-to-large, robust snake with a deep, rectangular-looking head. Its upper colour can range from pale fawn or yellowish brown to dark brown. The head and neck are usually noticeably darker than the body, and dark-edged scales can form a broken herringbone pattern along the back. The underside is yellowish with orange blotches, while the eye has a very dark iris and a round pupil. [1]

Colour is not a reliable way to decide what snake is in front of you. The Australian Museum reports seasonal change, with individuals becoming darker in winter and paler in summer. It also notes that, within the Inland Taipan’s range, the species may be confused with brown snakes including the Western Brown Snake and Eastern Brown Snake. [1] A distant sighting, a photograph, size estimate or time of day is therefore **not** a safe confirmation of species.

The alternative name “Fierce Snake” is especially unhelpful for judging an encounter. The NSW government profile says the name describes the venom rather than the snake’s temperament, and describes the species as shy and reclusive. That must not be taken as a reason to approach it: any snake can defend itself if pressured, cornered or handled. [2]

## Australian range and habitat

For an Inland Taipan in Australia, think **arid inland country**, not the coast. The Australian Museum places the species in the Channel Country of south-western Queensland and north-eastern South Australia. It records older reports from north-western Victoria and the Bourke area of New South Wales, but says the species has not been collected in either state since those nineteenth-century records. [1]

This history matters when reading distribution maps. The NSW threatened-species profile lists the species as **Extinct in NSW** and **not listed** at Commonwealth level. [2] A historical record does not show that a snake is presently established at that location. For a sighting or safety decision, treat the animal in front of you as an unidentified snake and keep clear rather than trying to fit it to an assumed range.

The species is associated with deep cracking clays and cracking loams of floodplains. It may also use nearby gibber plains, dunes and rocky outcrops when cover is available. Sparse chenopod shrubs, lignum and occasional eucalypts near water channels are characteristic vegetation described for these sites. [1] Deep soil cracks, crevices, holes and mammal burrows provide shelter from exposure. [1] These features explain why an animal may be hard to see and why reaching into holes, cracks or concealed spaces in suitable country is a poor choice.

WildGuardian’s **CRITICAL** label is this page’s database risk label; it is not a substitute for an official conservation listing. Available official and expert pages need to be read by jurisdiction: the Australian Museum lists the species as Rare in Queensland and presumed extinct in NSW and Victoria, while the NSW government profile gives its NSW and Commonwealth listings above. [1] [2]

## Activity and ecology

The best-supported description is that Inland Taipans are primarily, perhaps exclusively, active on the surface by day. The Australian Museum says they are most active in the earlier morning, when they briefly bask and forage in or near deep soil cracks and animal burrows before sheltering for the rest of the day. In cooler weather, they may also be active in the afternoon. [1] This is ecology, not a safety rule: do not use a time of day to decide a snake is harmless or to approach it.

In the wild, the species appears to feed entirely on small to medium mammals, particularly the Long-haired Rat (*Rattus villosissimus*), as well as House Mice and small dasyurids. [1] The University of Melbourne’s Australian Venom Research Unit likewise describes rats as its food and notes that it may live in their burrows, while stressing that relatively little is known about the snake in the wild. [5] That limitation is important: claims about a precise local routine, abundance or behaviour should not be assumed from a general species profile.

The Australian Museum describes the species as rarely encountered by the average person because its habitat is remote and its time above ground is brief. [1] Its rarity in everyday encounters does not reduce the need for space and caution in relevant inland habitat.

## Safer encounters with any unknown snake

The appropriate goal is separation, not identification. Healthdirect advises people who see a snake not to panic, to back away to a safe distance and let it move away; snakes often try to escape when disturbed. [3]

- **Stop and give the snake room.** Keep other people and pets away, and take a route that increases distance rather than trying to move, touch or corner the animal. [3]
- **Do not attempt to catch, kill or handle it.** Healthdirect and ANZCOR both warn against this; ANZCOR notes that attempts to kill a snake can lead to multiple bites or another person being bitten. [3] [4]
- **Do not pursue a photograph or an identification.** ANZCOR says a digital photograph can be helpful only if it is safe to obtain; it is never necessary for immediate care. [4]
- **Use local professional wildlife advice if a snake cannot be left to move away safely.** Keep clear while arranging help; do not turn a wildlife issue into a close encounter.

These principles apply even where a person believes the snake is an Inland Taipan. They avoid the central preventable risk: putting hands, feet or tools close to an animal that may bite in defence.

## Suspected bite or medical response

A bite can be painless and marks may be absent or difficult to see. ANZCOR lists possible signs such as headache, nausea or vomiting, abdominal pain, visual changes, difficulty speaking, swallowing or breathing, weakness and paralysis, but symptoms are not a reason to delay. [4] Healthdirect states that all snake bites are a medical emergency and that a person who thinks they have been bitten needs hospital assessment. [3]

Take these immediate actions for a suspected venomous snake bite:

1. **Call 000 for an ambulance.** Do this for a suspected bite, without waiting for identification. If Triple Zero does not work on a mobile phone, Healthdirect advises trying 112. [3]
2. **Keep the person still, reassured and under observation.** ANZCOR recommends pressure bandaging with immobilisation and directs responders to use its official technique guidance; follow instructions from emergency services and trained first-aid guidance rather than improvising. [4]
3. **Start CPR only if the person is unresponsive and not breathing normally.** [3] [4]

Do **not** wash the bite area, cut or incise it, apply an arterial tourniquet, suck the bite, or try to catch or kill the snake. [3] [4] Treatment decisions, including whether antivenom is needed, belong in hospital under clinical supervision. [3] The safe message is simple: **call 000 and follow official advice, even if you are unsure which snake was involved.**

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the Inland Taipan the same as the Fierce Snake?

Yes. *Oxyuranus microlepidotus* is called the Inland Taipan and Fierce Snake, as well as Small-scaled Snake and Lignum Snake. [1]

### Where does the Inland Taipan live in Australia?

The Australian Museum identifies its current core distribution as the Channel Country of south-western Queensland and north-eastern South Australia. It associates the species with floodplain cracking clays and nearby country with suitable cover. [1]

### Can I identify an Inland Taipan by its dark head or brown colour?

No. Those are reported features, but colour changes seasonally and the species may resemble brown snakes within its range. Keep a safe distance and do not seek a closer view to confirm an identification. [1]

### Does an old NSW or Victorian record mean the species is still there?

Not necessarily. The Australian Museum describes nineteenth-century records from those areas but says the species has not been collected in either state since then; the NSW profile lists it as Extinct in NSW. [1] [2]

### What should I do if an Inland Taipan, or any unknown snake, may have bitten someone?

**Call 000 immediately for an ambulance and follow official advice without waiting for identification.** Keep the person still, do not wash or interfere with the bite area, and do not attempt to catch the snake. [3] [4]
