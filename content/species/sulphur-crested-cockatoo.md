# Sulphur-Crested Cockatoo (*Cacatua galerita*)

The **Sulphur-Crested Cockatoo** is a large, conspicuous white cockatoo with a yellow crest. In Australia, the accepted taxon is *Cacatua (Cacatua) galerita*; WildGuardian uses the familiar scientific name *Cacatua galerita* on this page. It is a bird in the cockatoo family, Cacatuidae. [1]

This record carries a **LOW** WildGuardian risk label. That label is a database label, not a substitute for the legal or management settings that apply in a particular state, region or reserve. In New South Wales, for example, native birds are generally protected, while Sulphur-Crested Cockatoos have a specific local exception in several agricultural Local Land Services regions. [5]

## How to identify a Sulphur-Crested Cockatoo

An adult Sulphur-Crested Cockatoo is a large white parrot, generally **45–50 cm** long. Its most useful visible features are a dark grey-to-black bill, a prominent sulphur-yellow crest, and yellow colouring beneath the wings and tail. [2] [3]

The usual combination is more helpful than any single mark. A raised yellow crest is highly distinctive, but a responsible identification should also match the white body, dark bill and yellow underwing or undertail wash. Museums Victoria describes the bird as a conspicuous, noisy white cockatoo with a yellow crest; the Australian Museum likewise notes that it is readily conspicuous at rest and in flight. [2] [3]

Male and female birds look similar. At close range, females may show a red-brown iris and males a darker brown iris, but this is not a practical or necessary field check. Young birds also resemble adults. [2] [3] A loud, harsh screech is characteristic, yet sound alone should not be treated as proof of identity. [2]

## Australian range and habitat

The Australian Faunal Directory records *Cacatua (Cacatua) galerita* in New South Wales, the Northern Territory, Queensland, South Australia, Tasmania, Victoria and Western Australia. [1] The Australian Museum summarises its range as the northern and eastern mainland and Tasmania. [2]

Western Australia needs particular care in any range description. The WA Department of Primary Industries and Regional Development says the native range includes the Kimberley, while populations in the south-west are outside the bird’s natural range and stem from escaped or deliberately released captive birds. Its 2026 plan describes the native Australian range from the Kimberley east to Cape York and south to Tasmania, alongside introduced populations in south-west WA. [4]

This cockatoo uses a broad set of Australian habitats. Authoritative museum accounts list timbered habitats, forests, woodlands, grasslands and urban areas; it is also common around human settlements. [2] [3] That adaptability explains why people may encounter the species in established suburbs as well as in woodland and forest landscapes. It does not mean that every large white cockatoo seen in those places is this species.

## Behaviour and ecology

Sulphur-Crested Cockatoos are social birds. They commonly feed in small to large groups, with one or more birds positioned nearby as lookouts while others feed. [2] Their documented natural diet includes berries, seeds, nuts and roots; Museums Victoria also lists fruit and seeds. [2] [3]

Their sound is an important part of an encounter. The Australian Museum describes the common call as a loud screech with a slight upward inflection, while Museums Victoria describes loud, harsh calls that carry over distance. [2] [3] Noise by itself is not evidence that a bird is distressed or aggressive: it is part of the species’ normal communication.

The species is generally described as staying in the same area throughout the year. [2] Nesting occurs in suitable tree hollows. Both sexes prepare the hollow, incubate the eggs and care for the chicks, and young birds can remain with their parents through the year; family groups may remain together for long periods. [2] [3] The Australian Museum records breeding seasons of August to January in southern Australia and May to September in the north. [2]

A powerful bill is central to the bird’s ecology and is also why distance is sensible. The Australian Museum reports that birds may bite off small branches and leaves when not feeding, and notes the activity may help maintain bill length. [2] In urban settings, the same source records damage to timber decking and panelling. [2]

## Safer encounters with wild cockatoos

Enjoy the bird as a wild animal: pause, give it space and avoid deliberate close interaction. Its size, powerful bill and social behaviour make a calm, hands-off encounter the lowest-risk choice for people and the bird. The Australian Museum notes that cockatoos can be conspicuous, noisy and present in groups, so a clear view from a distance is usually all that is needed for appreciation or identification. [2]

If a bird appears sick, injured or orphaned, the appropriate next contact is a licensed wildlife rehabilitation provider or veterinarian. NSW Environment and Heritage says only licensed wildlife rehabilitation providers or registered veterinarians can take a sick, injured or orphaned native animal into care, and cautions that untrained response can harm people or wildlife. [6] Wildlife arrangements and contact pathways differ between states and territories, so use the relevant local authority or licensed rescue service rather than assuming the rules are the same everywhere.

Where the encounter is in south-west WA, avoid assuming the bird is part of its natural local range. The WA Government treats populations outside the native range there as a biosecurity issue and runs an eradication program. [4] Local authority guidance is the right source for any location-specific concern.

## Medical response after a bite or scratch

Cockatoos are not bats, and bat-exposure advice is not relevant to a cockatoo encounter. However, a bite or scratch that breaks skin is still a medical issue. SA Health says all animal bites and scratches should be washed immediately and thoroughly with soap and water, then have an antiseptic applied. [8]

SA Health advises medical assessment as soon as possible for deep puncture wounds, extensive superficial injuries, or more than superficial injuries to the hands, face or genitals. [8] Healthdirect also advises seeing a doctor as soon as possible if an animal bite has broken the skin; it identifies a large or deep wound, a facial or head wound, or bleeding that will not stop as reasons for emergency-department care. [7] This is general Australian health guidance, not a diagnosis; for immediate clinical advice, Healthdirect’s 24-hour helpline is 1800 022 222, and emergencies require 000. [7]

## Frequently asked questions

### What colour is a Sulphur-Crested Cockatoo?

It is chiefly white, with a sulphur-yellow crest and yellow tinge beneath the wings and tail. The bill and legs are dark grey to black. [2] [3]

### Where are Sulphur-Crested Cockatoos found in Australia?

Australian records span NSW, the NT, Queensland, South Australia, Tasmania, Victoria and WA. [1] They occur through northern and eastern mainland Australia and Tasmania, with an important distinction between native Kimberley occurrences and introduced south-west WA populations. [2] [4]

### Are Sulphur-Crested Cockatoos native to Western Australia?

They occur naturally in the Kimberley region of WA. South-west WA populations are outside the natural range and are managed by the WA Government as introduced birds. [4]

### Do Sulphur-Crested Cockatoos nest in tree hollows?

Yes. Both sexes prepare a suitable tree hollow, incubate the eggs and care for chicks. [2]

### What should I do if I find one that looks unwell?

Contact a local licensed wildlife rehabilitation provider or veterinarian for advice. NSW Environment and Heritage directs the public to professional help for sick, injured or orphaned native wildlife and notes that only licensed providers or registered veterinarians can take such animals into care. [6]
