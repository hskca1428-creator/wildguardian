# Box Jellyfish (*Chironex fleckeri*)

The Box Jellyfish in this profile is **one species**: *Chironex fleckeri*. It is also called the sea wasp. The Australian Museum places it in the family Chirodropidae and class Cubozoa, rather than treating “box jellyfish” as a single catch-all animal. [1] This distinction matters. Australia has other jellyfish, including other box jellyfish, but this CRITICAL WildGuardian record concerns *C. fleckeri* specifically.

In northern Australia, a suspected encounter with this animal demands a safety-first response. *C. fleckeri* has venomous tentacles and its sting can be life-threatening. Healthdirect advises that a jellyfish sting in tropical waters warrants a call to Triple Zero (000) for an ambulance. [4] The aim is not to identify an animal at close range. It is to keep people out of harm’s way, follow local beach advice and act promptly if a sting is suspected.

## Identification limits

An adult *Chironex fleckeri* is large and transparent, with a box-shaped bell and up to 60 tentacles arranged in four clumps around the bell’s base. The Australian Museum gives a maximum bell size of 30 centimetres and tentacles up to three metres. [1] The Northern Territory Government likewise notes that box jellyfish can appear colourless, making them difficult to see. [3]

Those features are useful background, not a safe field test. A clear or nearly invisible jellyfish may be hard to recognise in moving water, glare, surf or murky conditions. It is also unsafe to assume that an unidentified jellyfish is harmless because its shape or size does not match a photograph. Healthdirect says that, north of Bundaberg in Queensland and Geraldton in Western Australia, an unknown jellyfish sting should be treated as a box jellyfish sting. [5]

This page therefore does not offer a close-identification method or encourage inspection of jellyfish in the water or on the beach. In a tropical northern setting, uncertainty should lead to precaution and local safety advice, not delay.

## Australian range and habitat

The Australian Museum describes *C. fleckeri* as occupying tropical coastal waters from Western Australia, through the Northern Territory, to Queensland. [1] Within that broad northern range, local conditions matter. The Northern Territory Government describes shallow waters along the northern Australian coast and reports that, after local rain and in calm seas, box jellyfish may be numerous near river and creek outlets and around boat ramps. [3]

Seasonal risk messaging is also local rather than an all-clear calendar. Queensland Government states that dangerous tropical marine stingers occur in tropical Queensland waters all year, with higher risk during the November–May marine-stinger season. [6] In the Northern Territory, box jellyfish are more likely in waters from 1 October to 1 June, but stings have been recorded in every month of the year. [3] Visitors and residents should therefore treat local signage, beach closures and lifeguard advice as the decision point for a particular place and day.

Calm, inshore water is a relevant setting for this species. James Cook University describes the Australian box jellyfish as a large, active jellyfish common in the calm inshore waters of northern Australia. [2] That does not mean every calm beach is unsafe, or that rougher water is safe. It does mean that estuaries, creek mouths and protected coastal waters deserve the same attention as open beaches when local stinger warnings are active. [2] [3]

## Ecology and behaviour

*Chironex fleckeri* is an active predator, not simply a drifting blob. James Cook University’s research overview says its potent venom is adapted for capturing large, active prey such as prawns and fish. [2] The Australian Museum similarly reports that it is found most often in summer in inshore waters, where it feeds on bay-dwelling prawns, and may enter shallows on hot, cloudy, still days while seeking prey. [1]

Like other jellyfish, this species has more than the conspicuous adult form encountered in the water. James Cook University notes that its polyps are the longest-lived life stage and the source of the adult medusae, while also emphasising that little is known about the benthic polyp stage. [2] This is a useful limit on what can be claimed about where box jellyfish originate or how reliably their presence can be predicted. Researchers are developing environmental-DNA tools because direct observation of cubozoan jellyfish is difficult. [2]

For beach users, the practical point is simple: absence of a visible jellyfish is not proof of absence. Prefer the current advice provided at the beach over personal sighting or a remembered season date.

## Safer encounters in tropical Australia

Choose the controls that reduce exposure before entering the water. Queensland Government advises people in tropical Queensland to swim at patrolled beaches between the red-and-yellow flags, obey safety signs, remain out of closed beaches and ask lifesavers for advice. It also recommends using stinger nets where provided and considering a full-body lycra suit for protection from marine stings. [6]

Do not touch marine stingers washed up on the beach: Queensland Government warns that they can still sting. [6] Keep children close, especially in shallow water, and treat a warning sign or a lifeguard direction as applicable to everyone in the group. The Northern Territory Government identifies children as at greater risk from box-jellyfish stings because of their smaller body mass. [3]

These measures are not a guarantee that a sting cannot occur. They are practical ways to reduce exposure in the part of Australia where *C. fleckeri* is relevant. If a beach has no patrol, no net and no current local guidance, do not replace those safeguards with a visual search for jellyfish.

## Suspected box-jellyfish sting: immediate emergency action

A suspected *Chironex fleckeri* sting is an emergency. Get out of the water. [3] Healthdirect’s official guidance for a box jellyfish sting is:

> “Call triple zero (000) and ask for an ambulance. Get help from a lifesaver or lifeguard if possible. Start cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) if needed. Pour or spray vinegar onto the sting for at least 30 seconds. Then pick off the tentacles.” [4]

This quoted first-aid sequence is specifically for box jellyfish, Irukandji and other tropical stingers. If vinegar is not available, Healthdirect says to pick off the tentacles and wash the area with sea water. [4] It is the emergency exception to the general rule not to touch wildlife; do not delay the ambulance call or the emergency response while trying to determine the species.

A person with a major box-jellyfish sting may have severe pain and red whip-like lines, and severe envenomation can lead to cardiac arrest. [5] Once emergency help is being arranged, ongoing care belongs with ambulance and hospital clinicians. Healthdirect notes that hospital treatment may include antivenom, strong pain-relief medicines and heart monitoring. [5]

## Frequently asked questions

### Is every box-shaped jellyfish in Australia *Chironex fleckeri*?

No. This page is limited to *Chironex fleckeri*, a species in the family Chirodropidae. “Box jellyfish” can be used for more than one jellyfish, so a common-name match alone is not a reliable species identification. [1] In tropical northern Australia, a sting from an unknown jellyfish should be managed as a possible box-jellyfish sting where Healthdirect’s regional guidance applies. [5]

### Where in Australia is the Box Jellyfish found?

The documented Australian range is tropical coastal water from Western Australia through the Northern Territory to Queensland. [1] In the NT, shallow coastal water, creek and river outlets, and boat-ramp areas after rain and in calm seas are specifically noted settings. [3]

### Is there a safe month to ignore stinger warnings?

No. Tropical Queensland marine stingers are present all year, although risk is higher from November to May. [6] NT guidance says box-jellyfish stings have been recorded in all months. [3] Follow location-specific warnings and beach advice rather than relying on a calendar.

### What should I do if I see a jellyfish washed up on the beach?

Keep clear and do not touch it. Queensland Government warns that washed-up marine stingers can still sting. [6] Alert a lifesaver or the relevant local authority if one is available, particularly at a swimming area.

### Does the CRITICAL label mean the Box Jellyfish is threatened with extinction?

No. In this WildGuardian record, CRITICAL is a public-safety risk label. It is not a statement of conservation status, population trend or legal protection.
