# Mulga Snake (King Brown) — *Pseudechis australis*

> **Safety first — WildGuardian risk label: CRITICAL.** Treat any suspected Mulga Snake bite as a medical emergency. **Call 000 for an ambulance and follow official advice without waiting to identify the snake.** Australian guidance says all snake bites should be treated as potentially life-threatening. [2] [3]

The Mulga Snake, also called the King Brown Snake, is *Pseudechis australis*: a large Australian elapid in the genus *Pseudechis*. Despite its widely used “King Brown” name, it is not a brown snake in the scientific sense; the name can cause confusion, including in clinical contexts. [1] [4] For people outdoors, the useful point is simpler: a large brown, reddish-brown or dark snake cannot be safely named from a quick look. Give it space and do not turn identification into an encounter.

The Australian Museum currently recognises one Mulga Snake species, *Pseudechis australis*, while noting that molecular work supports the possibility of cryptic species within it. [1] This page uses the accepted name in the supplied record and focuses on safe, practical recognition of its place in Australia rather than trying to separate every local variation.

## Identification: useful clues, firm limits

An adult Mulga Snake is often robust, with a broad, deep head and noticeably full cheeks. Its scales can create a reticulated or net-like effect because they are often dark at the outer portion and paler at the base. Colour varies greatly: brown, reddish brown, coppery brown and brownish black are all described, while some far-northern arid animals have little dark pigment and southern animals can appear almost black. The underside may be cream to salmon-coloured. [1]

Those features are **descriptive, not a field test**. The Australian Museum says the species overlaps with similar-looking elapids, including brown snakes and other *Pseudechis*. [1] Lighting, distance, age, local variation and the animal’s posture all reduce the value of a visual impression. The common name also does not solve that problem: “King Brown” should not be used as a shortcut to decide what a snake is, how dangerous it may be, or what medical care is needed. [1] [4]

The safest identification standard is therefore behavioural: treat an unknown snake as a snake, stay well back, and let professionals assess any medical risk. Never approach for a better view, attempt to touch it, or try to make it display its markings.

## Australian range and habitat

The Mulga Snake has an exceptionally broad Australian distribution. The Australian Museum describes it as occurring across the continent except for the extreme southern and general south-eastern parts. [1] That broad statement is more useful than assuming that a sighting in a particular inland, northern or western landscape must be a Mulga Snake. Presence in a broad region is not proof of identity.

Its documented habitat range is equally wide. It occurs from closed tropical monsoon forest to *Triodia* hummock grassland, chenopod shrubland, gibber country and sandy desert; it may also occur in disturbed places such as wheat fields. [1] This is a snake that can use very different landscapes, so habitat alone is not a reliable identification tool.

When not active, Mulga Snakes may shelter in abandoned animal burrows, deep soil cracks, beneath fallen timber or large rocks, and in deep rock crevices or cavities. [1] These are useful facts for understanding why a snake may be encountered unexpectedly, not a reason to inspect such places. Keep hands and feet out of places you cannot see into, particularly when moving around bushland, rocks, timber or farm infrastructure.

## Behaviour and activity

Mulga Snakes are terrestrial and can be active by day, around dawn and dusk, and at night. The Australian Museum reports that activity changes with temperature, with reduced activity in the middle of the day and from midnight to dawn; in hot northern conditions, activity is often concentrated in the late evening and early hours after dusk. [1] The Australian Venom Research Unit likewise notes frequent night activity in hot weather. [4]

This flexible pattern is a reason not to rely on a rule such as “snakes only come out during the day” or “a snake at night cannot be a Mulga.” Use a torch outdoors after dark, move deliberately, and take particular care before placing hands where they cannot be seen. Healthdirect also advises giving a snake a safe distance and allowing it to move away, as snakes often seek to escape disturbance. [2]

In the wild, the species eats a broad range of vertebrate prey, including frogs, reptiles and their eggs, birds and mammals; it also sometimes eats invertebrates and carrion. [1] This broad diet fits its broad habitat use. It does **not** mean that a snake near a house, shed, water source or food for rodents can be identified as a Mulga Snake.

A snake’s response when disturbed should never be tested. The Australian Museum reports that temperament can vary by locality. It describes defensive postures such as inflating the body, holding the head and flattened neck in a wide curve, hissing and, if further pressed, striking. [1] The appropriate response is to stop, create distance if it is safe to do so, and leave the snake an unobstructed path away—not to corner it, provoke it or attempt to move it.

## Safer encounters at home, on Country and outdoors

Most safety decisions can be made without knowing the snake’s name. Slow down in places where visibility is poor, especially long grass, leaf litter, timber, rocky ground and around buildings. At night, use a torch rather than stepping into darkness. Healthdirect lists bushland, long grass, leaf litter, sheds and houses among places where snakes may be found or hide. [2]

If you see a snake, use these **simple safety steps**:

- **Stop and keep well back.** Do not approach, touch, block or try to frighten the animal.
- **Give it room to leave.** A calm retreat creates less pressure on both you and the snake; Healthdirect advises backing away to a safe distance and letting a snake move away on its own. [2]
- **Keep other people and animals clear.** Warn others nearby without directing them closer to look.
- **Do not catch, kill or handle it.** Healthdirect and ANZCOR both warn against attempts to catch or kill a snake; doing so increases the risk of further bites. [2] [3]

If a snake is in a setting where people cannot maintain a safe distance, do not improvise a removal. Keep the area clear and seek suitable local professional assistance. Do not rely on colour, a photo taken at close range, or a neighbour’s identification to decide whether it is safe.

## Bite or possible bite: act before identification

A suspected bite from a Mulga Snake—or from **any** snake—needs urgent medical assessment. A bite may be painless and may leave no obvious marks, so an absence of pain or visible punctures is not a reason to wait. [3] Healthdirect says that if you think someone has been bitten, they need hospital assessment even if they seem well. [2]

**Call 000 immediately and ask for an ambulance. Do not wait for a name, a photograph or symptoms to develop.** [2] [3] While help is being arranged, follow current official Australian snake-bite advice: keep the person still, calm and under observation, and use pressure bandaging with immobilisation in line with that advice. [3] If the person is unresponsive and not breathing normally, ANZCOR directs bystanders to begin CPR. [3]

Do not wash the bite area, cut it, suck it, use an arterial tourniquet, or attempt to catch or kill the snake. [2] [3] These actions can create harm or complicate care. Antivenom decisions are made by clinicians in an appropriately equipped medical facility; they are not something to infer from a common name or a visual identification. [2] [3]

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a Mulga Snake the same as a King Brown Snake?

Yes. The Australian Museum lists King Brown Snake as an alternative name for *Pseudechis australis*. [1] The accepted scientific name is the clearest way to refer to the species, because common names can confuse rather than confirm identification.

### Is the Mulga Snake actually a brown snake?

No. *Pseudechis australis* belongs to the genus *Pseudechis*, and the University of Melbourne’s Australian Venom Research Unit specifically cautions that the “King Brown” name can lead to confusion. [4] Do not base medical or safety decisions on the word “brown” in its common name.

### Where in Australia might Mulga Snakes occur?

The Australian Museum describes a distribution across Australia except for the extreme southern and general south-eastern parts of the continent. [1] Because its range and habitat use are broad, a location alone cannot identify an individual snake.

### Can I identify one from its colour or a quick photo?

Not reliably. Mulga Snakes vary from pale brown through reddish and coppery tones to very dark animals, and the species overlaps with similar-looking elapids. [1] Keep your distance rather than seeking a closer view or a photo for identification.

### What should I do if I think a Mulga Snake has bitten someone?

Treat it as a suspected snake bite: **call 000 for an ambulance immediately and follow official advice without waiting for identification.** [2] [3] Keep the person still and do not attempt home treatment or snake capture. [2] [3]
