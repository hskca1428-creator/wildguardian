# Saltwater Crocodile (*Crocodylus porosus*)

**WildGuardian profile:** [Saltwater Crocodile](/wildlife/saltwater-crocodile) (ID 18)
**Risk label:** **CRITICAL** — an encounter-risk label for this WildGuardian profile, not a single Australia-wide conservation classification.

The Saltwater Crocodile is the common Australian name for *Crocodylus porosus*, the accepted scientific name in the Australian Faunal Directory. “Estuarine crocodile” is another established common name, so a source using either name can be referring to the same species. The supplied database name is current; no taxonomic correction is needed. [1] [3]

> **Safety:** In crocodile country, do not treat clear-looking water, a lack of a sighting or the absence of a warning sign as proof that a waterway is safe. Estuarine crocodiles can use freshwater as well as coastal water and may remain concealed. [3] [5]

## Identification: useful clues, not a safety test

An adult Saltwater Crocodile has a heavy build, a broad snout and a generally broad, knobbly head. Its colouring may range from almost black through grey and olive-brown, with dark mottling. The jaw line looks uneven because its teeth are irregular in size. Queensland’s official profile says adults grow beyond 4 metres; it gives an average male length of 3–4 metres and notes that females rarely exceed 3.5 metres. [2]

Australia has two crocodile species. The other, the Freshwater Crocodile (*Crocodylus johnstoni*), is generally lighter-built and has a noticeably narrower, longer snout. These are helpful comparison points, but they are not a reason to move closer for an identification. Juveniles, distance, glare, murky water and a partly submerged animal can make a visual call unreliable. Treat any crocodile in or beside a northern Australian waterway with the same caution rather than trying to identify it at close range. [3] [5]

“Saltwater” is also a misleadingly narrow name. The species can occur in tidal rivers and estuaries, beaches and offshore islands, but also in freshwater lagoons, rivers and swamps far inland. [2] [7]

## Australian range and habitat

The Australian Faunal Directory records *C. porosus* in the Northern Territory, Queensland and Western Australia. [1] In Queensland, the state government describes its known occurrence from Gladstone to Cape York Peninsula and throughout the Gulf of Carpentaria. [2] An Australian Museum profile describes the broader Australian coastal extent as from Broome in north-western Western Australia to the Gladstone area, while the Northern Territory’s current management program describes use of marine, coastal, estuarine, floodplain and riverine areas. [3] [7]

For people planning a visit, the important point is not to turn those descriptions into a sharp local boundary. Western Australia’s parks guide warns that crocodiles can be in any waterway in crocodile country, including freshwater, and that no warning sign does not establish that swimming or other water recreation is safe. Follow current signs, closures and advice from the park manager or wildlife authority for the place you are visiting. [5]

## Behaviour and ecology

Saltwater Crocodiles are ambush predators. Queensland’s official species profile describes them waiting at the water’s edge, then lunging or snapping at animals that approach to drink or feed. Their diet broadens markedly as they grow: young animals take small aquatic prey, while adults can overpower much larger animals. [2]

They do not need to be seen for a risk to exist. Queensland’s 2026 public-safety guidance says crocodiles use stealth and ambush tactics, are most active at night, dusk and dawn, and may be especially mobile during and after high tides and heavy rain. [4] In Queensland, courtship begins around October and November, and nesting occurs from December to April; the female guards a nest of vegetation and soil near water. This is regional breeding information, not a timetable for deciding whether a place is safe. [2]

The species is part of northern Australian ecosystems, while its presence also creates a serious public-safety issue around waterways. The Northern Territory’s current program explicitly manages both conservation and risks to people, including through the Be Crocwise awareness program. [7]

## Safer encounters in crocodile country

The aim is to avoid creating a close water-edge encounter. Use the rules below alongside local directions, which take priority.

- Keep well back from the water’s edge. Queensland advises at least 5 metres, with a solid object between a person and the water if moving closer is unavoidable. [4]
- Keep children closely supervised and pets on a lead and away from the edge; avoid low, small watercraft such as kayaks and paddleboards in crocodile habitat. [4]
- Keep food, fish scraps and offal away from people and campsites. Food associations can change crocodile behaviour and create risk. [4] [5]
- When boating, keep arms, legs and other body parts inside the vessel. If a crocodile approaches a boat, increase separation rather than engaging with it. [5]
- Do not wade at the water’s edge to free fishing gear. The Australian Museum advises cutting a tangled line rather than entering the water. [3]
- Keep clear of crocodile traps and comply with warning signs, temporary closures and directions from rangers or wildlife officers. [4]

Do not approach, touch or attempt to manage a crocodile. State and territory agencies have their own reporting and management arrangements; for example, Western Australia identifies crocodile control zones in Broome and Kununurra and responds to reports there. [5]

## Injury or medical response

A crocodile bite or attack is a traumatic animal injury, not a venom exposure. It needs emergency and wound care rather than snakebite-specific first aid. Healthdirect advises urgent medical help for wounds caused by an animal bite, and says a doctor or nurse should assess a bite wound within 2–6 hours. [6]

Call Triple Zero (000) for an ambulance immediately for severe bleeding, loss of consciousness, faintness, confusion, chest pain, shortness of breath, or a deep wound that will not stop bleeding with pressure. While waiting, Healthdirect advises firm direct pressure with a clean pad, dressing or dry cloth to control severe bleeding. If an object is embedded, do not remove it; put pressure around it instead. [6]

Even where a wound appears smaller, seek medical assessment promptly because animal bites and contaminated wounds can need clinical care. For non-emergency Australian health advice, Healthdirect is available on 1800 022 222. [6]

## FAQs

### Is an estuarine crocodile the same as a Saltwater Crocodile?

Yes. *Crocodylus porosus* is commonly called both the Saltwater Crocodile and the Estuarine Crocodile in Australian sources. [1] [3]

### Does the name mean it only lives in saltwater?

No. It is often found in tidal waters, but official Australian sources also record it in freshwater rivers, swamps, lagoons and billabongs, sometimes far inland. [2] [3] [7]

### Can I tell a Saltwater Crocodile from a Freshwater Crocodile safely from the bank?

A broad snout and heavy build suggest Saltwater Crocodile, while a longer, narrower snout suggests Freshwater Crocodile. But a distant or partly submerged animal is not reliably identifiable enough to change how carefully you behave. Maintain distance from either species. [3]

### Does WildGuardian’s CRITICAL label mean the species is nationally critically endangered?

No. On this page, CRITICAL communicates encounter risk. Conservation listings vary by jurisdiction: Queensland lists the species as vulnerable, while the Northern Territory program identifies it as a protected species listed as least concern there and as a marine and migratory species under the national EPBC Act. [4] [7]
