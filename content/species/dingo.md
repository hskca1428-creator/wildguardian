# Dingo — *Canis familiaris* (dingo)

The dingo is Australia’s wild dog and its only native canid. Queensland Government and the Australian Museum currently use *Canis familiaris* (dingo) as the scientific classification. [1] [2] This page remains the linkable [WildGuardian **Dingo database profile, ID 20**](/wildlife/dingo); the supplied database name, *Canis lupus dingo*, is retained as legacy record information rather than the name used in this page.

> **Safety at a glance — Medium encounter risk:** Dingoes are wild animals. Keep your distance, never feed or deliberately interact with one, and do not run if one approaches. Human food can make dingoes lose their wariness of people and can contribute to dangerous behaviour. [1]

## Name, classification and identification limits

The accepted page name is *Canis familiaris* (dingo), not *Canis lupus dingo*. This reflects a 2024 classification update reported by the Arthur Rylah Institute and the Australian Museum; Queensland Government likewise calls *Canis familiaris* (dingo) the current scientific name. [1] [2] [3] “Dingo” remains the useful common name for this Australian wildlife profile.

From a respectful distance, a dingo is dog-like, with a relatively broad head, erect ears, a tapered muzzle, short body fur and a bushy tail. The Australian Museum describes ginger with white feet as a typical coat, while noting golden-yellow coats in desert country and darker tan to black in forests. [2] Queensland Government also describes lean animals with permanently pricked ears, and says colour can vary across Australian populations. [1]

Those features are for observation, not close checking. A dingo can resemble a domestic dog, and coat colour and body condition vary. Queensland Government stresses that, despite the resemblance, dingoes are wild, opportunistic and unpredictable. [1] Stay back rather than trying to confirm an animal’s identity or ancestry at close range.

## Australian range and habitat

At a broad scale, dingoes occur across mainland Australia: Queensland Government says they are found in every state and habitat except Tasmania. [1] This does **not** mean that dingoes occur evenly across every part of those states. The Australian Museum reports that pastoral-era removal reduced their range and that they are mostly absent from many parts of New South Wales, Victoria, south-eastern South Australia and the southernmost part of Western Australia. [2] For a specific park, community or trip, follow local land-manager advice rather than treating a national description as a site map.

Dingoes are highly adaptable. Woodland and grassland close to forest edges are preferred habitat, and in dry country reliable water limits where they can live. [1] [2] Queensland Government describes a range from deserts to rainforests and notes that animals can travel long distances for water and food. [1]

Their status and management are not the same everywhere. For example, Queensland recognises dingoes as native wildlife and protects them in most national parks, while applying different biosecurity rules outside national parks. [1] On K’gari, free-ranging dingoes are known as **wongari** in the local Butchulla language and are especially significant because they have rarely interbred with domestic or feral dogs. [1] Local rules matter: do not assume that a rule or contact method from one state applies nationally.

## Behaviour and ecological role

Dingoes are Australia’s largest land-based carnivores and are an apex predator in the ecosystems they occupy. [1] They are opportunistic generalist predators: their food can include small and medium native mammals, birds and reptiles, introduced animals and domestic animals; they may also eat fish, crustaceans, insects, eggs and fruit, and scavenge carcasses. [1] What they eat changes with what is available, so a single sighting near a beach, track, camp or road does not reliably explain the animal’s usual diet or condition.

They are social animals that may hunt alone or in packs according to prey size. Queensland Government describes them as mainly crepuscular, hunting most often around dusk and dawn. [1] The Australian Museum also records territorial behaviour and communication by howling and scent marking. [2]

Food left by people is a safety and conservation problem. Queensland Government says discarded food, unsecured camp food, fish and bait can be eaten by dingoes; feeding or food left accessible can teach them to associate people with food and reduce their natural fear of people. [1] Interbreeding with domestic and feral dogs is also identified as a threat to the dingo’s genetic distinctiveness, particularly in more populated mainland areas. [1]

## Safer encounters with dingoes

Most useful safety actions are simple and preventive:

- **Never feed a dingo or leave food, bait or rubbish available.** Secure food and scented items as local park directions require. [1] [4]
- **Keep children within arm’s reach and stay with other people.** Queensland’s K’gari guidance specifically asks visitors to do this, including with teenagers. [1] [4]
- **Do not run or jog near dingoes.** Queensland guidance warns that running can trigger a negative interaction or chase response. [1] [4]
- **Use the rules for the place you are visiting.** On K’gari, visitors are asked to keep at least 20 metres—four car lengths—from dingoes, and families with children under 14 are strongly recommended to use fenced camping. [4]

If a dingo approaches or you feel threatened on K’gari, Queensland Parks and Wildlife Service advises people to face the animal rather than turn their back, make themselves look large, call loudly for help, and calmly back away toward a car or fenced area. If there is no safe place to retreat to, stand your ground and keep calling for help. [4] A dingo safety stick is a distance tool in that setting; the agency says it must not be used to harm or provoke a protected dingo. [4]

Call **000** in an emergency. [1] [4] On K’gari, report concerning interactions such as circling, lunging, nipping, biting or chasing to a ranger using the current contact route published by Queensland Parks and Wildlife Service. [4] Elsewhere, report a serious wildlife safety concern through the relevant park or land manager.

## If someone is bitten or injured

Treat a bite that breaks the skin as a medical matter, even if it seems small. Healthdirect says infections can occur after an animal bite and recommends seeing a doctor as soon as possible if the skin is broken. [5] This is ordinary animal-bite care, not snake or spider first aid.

For a minor wound while arranging medical advice, Healthdirect advises cleaning the skin around the wound with soap and water, using running water to remove dirt, hair or teeth if present, patting it dry with a clean cloth, and covering it with a clean dressing. Apply pressure with a dry dressing after cleaning if it is bleeding heavily. [5] A clinician can assess the wound, infection risk and whether a tetanus booster is needed. [5]

Go to an emergency department, or call **000** for an ambulance, if the wound is large or deep, the bite is on the face or head, or bleeding cannot be stopped. [5] Seek urgent reassessment for increasing pain, redness, swelling, warmth, pus or discharge, fever, or swollen lymph nodes, which Healthdirect lists as possible infection signs. [5]

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the dingo’s scientific name *Canis lupus dingo*?

No. This page uses the currently stated Australian classification, *Canis familiaris* (dingo). Queensland Government, the Australian Museum and Victoria’s Arthur Rylah Institute all use that classification. [1] [2] [3] The WildGuardian ID 20 profile preserves the supplied name as legacy database information so the record remains linkable.

### Are dingoes found in Tasmania?

No. Queensland Government’s national overview says dingoes occur in every Australian state and habitat except Tasmania. Their distribution on the mainland is patchy in some regions, so consult local information for an individual destination. [1] [2]

### Are dingoes just domestic dogs living wild?

They may look similar to domestic dogs, but Queensland Government describes dingoes as wild animals and says they are unpredictable. [1] The safe response to a dog-like canid in dingo country is distance and no interaction, not an attempt to determine its ancestry by appearance.

### May I feed a dingo to keep it away or get a photo?

No. Feeding or making food available can condition dingoes to seek food from people and may contribute to dangerous behaviour. It is also an offence to feed or deliberately interact with dingoes on K’gari. [1] [4]

### What should I do if a dingo bite breaks the skin?

Clean and cover the wound as directed above, seek medical advice as soon as possible, and call 000 for severe bleeding, a large or deep wound, or a bite to the face or head. [5] Report the encounter to the relevant ranger or land manager where requested; K’gari has specific reporting guidance. [4]
