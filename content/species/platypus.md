# Platypus (*Ornithorhynchus anatinus*)

The **platypus** is an Australian semi-aquatic mammal with a broad, flat tail, a soft duck-like bill and webbed front feet. Its scientific name is *Ornithorhynchus anatinus*. It belongs to the order Monotremata: mammals that lay eggs. [1] [2] WildGuardian’s record-level risk label for this animal is **LOW**.

A sighting can be a quiet privilege rather than a close encounter. Platypuses are shy, freshwater-dependent animals; a brief wake, ripples or a low shape on the water can be all that is visible. Queensland’s wildlife guidance notes that only about 10 per cent of the furred body may show when one floats low in the water. [3] Giving it space protects both the animal and the moment.

## Identification: what you can and cannot confirm

A platypus has dense, waterproof dark-brown to reddish-brown fur, a streamlined body, a broad flat tail, short legs and webbed feet. Its bill is sensitive rather than hard like a bird’s beak. The eyes and ear openings close under water. [1] Its low profile, usually at dawn, dusk or after dark, means a distant observation may not provide a reliable view of every feature. [1] [3]

Do not identify an animal from ripples alone. Water rats, turtles and water dragons can also be seen in or near Australian waterways; Queensland Government guidance highlights these as common cases of mistaken identity. [3] A platypus usually makes a small, quiet dive and may leave a modest pool of ripples or a bow wave from its front feet. [3] These are useful clues, not a reason to approach.

Sex cannot be safely inferred from a fleeting wildlife sighting. The important safety distinction is that **males have a sharp spur on each hind ankle**, linked to a venom gland in the thigh. [1] [3] The spur is not an identification feature to seek at close range. If the animal is seen only briefly or partly submerged, record the sighting simply as a possible platypus rather than claiming certainty.

## Australian range and freshwater habitat

The platypus is endemic to Australia and occurs in freshwater systems across much of eastern Australia and Tasmania. The Australian Museum records it in eastern Queensland and New South Wales, eastern, central and south-western Victoria, and throughout Tasmania; its range is not continuous in every catchment. [1] Queensland Government similarly describes a span from far north Queensland to Tasmania, with Queensland populations east of the Great Dividing Range and in some west-flowing streams. [3]

It is a freshwater species, not a marine animal. Platypuses use creeks, rivers, lakes connected to rivers and some built water storages. [3] They need more than water alone: a suitable waterway can include earth banks for burrows, bank vegetation that provides shade and cover, and logs, roots or coarse streambed material that supports the small invertebrates they eat. [1]

Out of the water, the animal spends much of its time in burrows in riverbanks, typically with entrances above water level. [2] That dependence on banks and flowing or standing freshwater explains why damage to waterway condition matters. Pollution, algal growth, siltation and loss of creek-bank plants place pressure on platypus habitat in Queensland. [3] NSW also identifies habitat loss, altered flows from dams, discarded fishing line and yabby traps as threats. [2]

## Ecology: built for life below the surface

The platypus is the only aquatic member of the monotreme group. Like other monotremes it lays eggs, but it is still a mammal and females feed their young with milk. [4] This is not an animal that feeds by sight under water: when foraging it closes its eyes, ears and nostrils, and uses receptors in the bill to detect prey-related signals. [1] [3]

Its food is mainly small aquatic invertebrates. Insect larvae are a major part of the diet, alongside freshwater shrimp, crayfish and other small water animals. [1] [3] Food can be collected in cheek pouches, then ground with horny jaw plates at the surface because adult platypuses have no teeth. [1] [3]

Activity is often concentrated around dusk, dawn and night, although it varies with place, weather, food and disturbance. [1] [3] A platypus may shelter in a bank burrow during the day, which is one reason that the absence of a visible animal does not show that a waterway is unused. [1]

## Safer encounters near Australian waterways

Watch from well back, stay quiet and let the animal choose its own path. Platypuses have sensitive hearing and are readily disturbed; early morning and dusk are the times Queensland Government identifies as most likely for seeing one. [3] A respectful observation is therefore still, quiet and brief rather than a pursuit for a closer photograph.

Never touch a platypus or try to move it. A startled animal may defend itself, and male hind-leg spurs can deliver venom. [1] [2] If an animal appears entangled, injured or stranded, keep people and pets clear and contact the relevant state or territory wildlife authority or a licensed wildlife rescue organisation; do not attempt an intervention yourself.

Small choices at waterways also matter. NSW identifies discarded fishing line and yabby traps as drowning risks, while Museums Victoria explains that loops and rings of litter can entangle a platypus and cause serious injury. [2] [4] Take all line, hooks, nets and litter away from the waterway, and help retain natural vegetation along creek banks. [3]

## If a platypus spur injury occurs

Platypus envenomation is uncommon, but it is not trivial. A 2025 peer-reviewed Australian clinical report describes severe local pain and swelling after penetrating spur injuries, along with a risk of deep tissue infection. [5] Seek urgent medical assessment after a suspected spur injury, particularly for a puncture wound or severe pain.

Do **not** apply snakebite-style pressure bandage immobilisation to a platypus spur injury. The clinical report finds no evidence to support it for platypus envenomation, whose symptoms are localised. [5] Medical professionals should determine pain treatment and wound management. In a medical emergency, call **000**. [7]

## Conservation context in Australia

A short database label cannot describe every jurisdiction’s legal or conservation setting. Queensland’s WildNet entry lists *O. anatinus* as **Special Least Concern** under Queensland’s nature conservation framework and as **not listed** under the EPBC field shown in that database. [6] In contrast, Museums Victoria reports that the platypus was listed as threatened in Victoria in 2021. [4] Local threats and protections therefore matter alongside any broad label.

For people who share a catchment with platypuses, the most useful response is prevention: protect bank vegetation, avoid leaving entangling debris near water, and reduce disturbance around known freshwater habitat. These actions align with the habitat pressures and entanglement risks documented by Australian government and museum sources. [2] [3] [4]

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a platypus a mammal?

Yes. The platypus is a mammal in the monotreme group. Monotremes are unusual mammals because they lay eggs. [1] [2]

### Where do platypuses live in Australia?

They live in freshwater systems in much of eastern Australia and Tasmania, including creeks, rivers and some lakes. Their presence can vary between catchments, so a regional range description is not proof that a particular waterway has platypuses. [1] [3]

### What does a platypus eat?

It mainly eats aquatic invertebrates, especially insect larvae, and also takes prey such as freshwater shrimp and crayfish. [1] [3]

### Are platypuses dangerous to people?

They are shy and usually avoid people, but a male has venomous hind-leg spurs and a spur injury can cause severe local pain and swelling. Do not touch or corner a platypus. [1] [2] [5]

### Why should fishing line and litter be removed from waterways?

Platypuses can become entangled in discarded line, traps and looped litter, with drowning or serious injury documented as risks. [2] [4]
