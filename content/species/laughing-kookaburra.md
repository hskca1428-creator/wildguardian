# Laughing Kookaburra

**Scientific name:** *Dacelo novaeguineae*
**Animal group:** Bird
**WildGuardian risk label:** LOW

The Laughing Kookaburra is a large Australian kingfisher known for a brown-and-white plumage pattern and a loud, cackling territorial call. It is a bird of places with trees, including forests, open woodland and some parks and gardens across its Australian range. [1] [2]

## Taxonomy and identification limits

The Australian Faunal Directory-backed Atlas of Living Australia record accepts this bird as *Dacelo (Dacelo) novaeguineae* (Hermann, 1783), with Laughing Kookaburra as its preferred common name. This page uses the shorter scientific name, *Dacelo novaeguineae*, used in the WildGuardian record and by Australian wildlife agencies. [3] [1]

Adults are about 40–45 centimetres long. Look for off-white underparts with faint dark-brown barring, brown back and wings, a rufous tail broadly barred with black, and a conspicuous dark-brown stripe through the eye. [1] The Victorian wildlife factsheet describes the bird as up to 46 centimetres from bill tip to tail tip and notes its large head and long beak. [4]

Identification from a single feature has limits. A quick view of a large brown-and-white bird, or hearing a laugh-like call, is not enough for a confident sighting in every setting. Check several features together—plumage, face pattern, tail, size, call and location—and use a local field guide or an experienced observer if the identification matters. The Australian Museum itself flags that the species has a similar-looking relative. [1]

## Australian range and habitat

In Australia, Laughing Kookaburras occur throughout the east. Victorian Government guidance describes the range as extending from Cape York Peninsula in Queensland to the Eyre Peninsula in South Australia; the species has also been introduced to Tasmania and south-western Western Australia. [4] The Australian Museum likewise describes an eastern Australian distribution and records the introductions to Tasmania and the extreme south-west of Western Australia. [1]

The species uses forests, open woodlands and the edges of plains, provided there is food through the year and a suitable nest site. [2] It can also occur in places with appropriate trees in parks and gardens. [1] In Victoria, habitat is described as sclerophyll woodland and open forest, with trees large enough for nests and open areas for hunting. [4]

For people looking for one, a bird sitting still on a branch or powerline may be scanning the ground. Habitat is a useful clue, but not proof of identity: use the field marks as well. [4]

## Behaviour and ecology

The familiar “laugh” is not evidence of amusement. It is a territorial call that warns other birds away, and the longer call may be delivered in a chorus with other individuals. [1] [2] This vocal behaviour is why the species can be noticed before it is seen.

Laughing Kookaburras take a broad range of animal prey. Authoritative Australian accounts list insects, worms and crustaceans as important foods, alongside small snakes, mammals, frogs and birds. [1] From a perch with a clear view, a bird waits and then pounces on prey below. Small prey can be swallowed whole; larger prey may be struck against a branch, rock or ground before it is eaten. [1] [2]

Tree hollows are central to breeding. The nest may be a bare chamber in a natural hollow or a burrow in a tree-dwelling termite mound. Both sexes share incubation and care of the young, while older offspring can help the breeding pair during the season. [1] NSW guidance also describes a family system in which the dominant pair breeds and other family members assist with incubation, feeding and protection. [2]

This dependence on suitable trees has practical consequences. NSW Environment and Heritage identifies loss of nesting, roosting and perch trees as a human-caused pressure, and warns that pesticides can move from contaminated insects into kookaburras. [2]

## Safer, wildlife-friendly encounters

Enjoy a Laughing Kookaburra from a respectful distance and let it hunt, perch and call without being drawn closer. Binoculars are a better way to see wildlife than creating a close encounter. [5]

**Do not feed kookaburras.** The Australian Museum notes that they can become tame around people and accept meat scraps. [1] NSW National Parks and Wildlife Service advises that human food can make native animals sick, create reliance on handouts and alter normal behaviour; hand-fed wildlife can also become aggressive when food is withheld. [5]

Avoid leaving food or scraps where birds can access them. If a kookaburra is repeatedly striking a window, the Victorian wildlife agency notes that this can happen during the September–January breeding season when birds attack their reflections, and that feeding encourages the behaviour. [6]

If a native bird appears injured or its behaviour creates a safety concern, seek advice from the relevant wildlife authority or local wildlife rescue organisation rather than trying to manage the animal yourself. In Victoria, the Department of Energy, Environment and Climate Action provides a tool to locate wildlife carers and rescue organisations. [7] Victorian law also protects native wildlife: killing, trapping or disturbing wildlife without a permit is illegal there. For situation-specific legal advice outside Victoria, ask the relevant state or territory authority. [8]

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the Laughing Kookaburra really laughing?

Get medical advice (Poisons Information Centre: 13 11 26). WildGuardian does not write first-aid instructions; follow the official guidance linked in the section above. [9] [10]

### Where are Laughing Kookaburras found in Australia?

They occur across eastern Australia, from Cape York Peninsula to the Eyre Peninsula according to Victorian Government guidance. They were introduced to Tasmania and south-western Western Australia. [4]

### What does a Laughing Kookaburra eat?

Its prey includes insects, worms and crustaceans as well as small vertebrates such as frogs, snakes, mammals and birds. It typically watches from a perch before pouncing. [1]

### Why is it important to retain suitable trees?

Trees provide nest hollows, roosting places and hunting perches. NSW Environment and Heritage identifies removal of these trees as reducing breeding and feeding sites. [2]

### What should I do if an injured kookaburra needs help?

Contact the appropriate state wildlife authority or a licensed wildlife rescue organisation for advice. Victoria’s official service, for example, directs people to nearby wildlife carers and rehabilitation organisations. [7]
