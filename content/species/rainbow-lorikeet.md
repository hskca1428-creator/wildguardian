# Rainbow Lorikeet (*Trichoglossus moluccanus*)

The Rainbow Lorikeet is a colourful Australian parrot of the family Psittaculidae. This page uses the accepted record name *Trichoglossus moluccanus*, which is also the scientific name given by the Australian Museum and NSW Department of Education material. [1] [2] **WildGuardian risk label: LOW.** That label is for this database record; it is not a conservation assessment or a statement of legal status in every Australian jurisdiction.

A flash of green wings, a blue to mauve head, an orange-yellow breast and a bright red beak makes this species one of the most recognisable birds in many Australian towns and cities. The Australian Museum gives a size range of 28–32 cm and notes that males and females look alike. [1] Their frequent screeching and chattering, fast flights and dusk roosts often make them noticeable before they are seen. [1]

## Identification: useful signs and important limits

Look for a medium-sized, long-tailed parrot with a predominantly green back, wings and tail; blue or mauve head and belly; orange-yellow breast; and red beak. These features are most useful when considered together, rather than as a colour checklist from one distant glimpse. [1] The NSW education fact sheet likewise describes blue heads, orange-yellow chests and green wings and backs, and identifies the species as a parrot. [2]

The Australian Museum lists the Scaly-breasted Lorikeet as a similar species. [1] Young Rainbow Lorikeets may be slightly duller than adults, according to Western Australia’s Department of Primary Industries and Regional Development. [3] Light, distance, movement and a partial view can all limit what a person can safely conclude from colour alone. [1] When identification matters, record only non-invasive observations such as the location, time, habitat and visible plumage, rather than approaching the bird.

A Rainbow Lorikeet is not simply “a rainbow-coloured parrot.” The scientific name matters because Australia has several lorikeet species, and colour overlap or a brief view can lead to an overconfident identification. [1] Treat an uncertain sighting as uncertain.

## Australian range and habitat

Rainbow Lorikeets occur in coastal northern and eastern Australia. The Australian Museum also records a local Perth population that began with aviary releases. [1] In eastern and northern Australia, NSW material places them in forests, woodlands, mangroves, coastal heath and urban areas. [2] Their use of well-treed suburbs, parks and gardens is consistent with the Australian Museum’s description of rainforest, woodlands and well-treed urban habitat. [1]

The Perth population needs separate context. Western Australia’s DPIRD says Rainbow Lorikeets have been established in Perth since 1968 after fewer than 10 birds escaped or were released. [3] DPIRD describes them as declared pests in Western Australia south of the Kimberley, including metropolitan Perth, and states that no Rainbow Lorikeets may be legally released into the wild in southern WA. [3] That jurisdiction-specific regulatory status should not be projected onto the species’ natural range in eastern and northern Australia.

Within their habitat, flowering trees and shrubs are central resources. Large trees with hollows are also important nesting sites. [2] This helps explain why a lorikeet can be familiar in a city street while still depending on connected tree cover, flowers and suitable hollows. [1] [2]

## Life in flowering trees

Rainbow Lorikeets mostly forage in the flowers of trees and shrubs for nectar and pollen. They also eat fruit, seeds and some insects. [1] A brush-tipped tongue helps them collect nectar and pollen. [2] As a lorikeet moves between flowers, pollen can be carried between plants; the NSW source identifies the species as an important pollinator of many Australian plants. [2]

They are social birds. Loud, fast-moving flocks and communal roosts at dusk are characteristic observations, while the Australian Museum describes the species as largely sedentary with some nomadic movement in response to seasonal flowering or fruiting. [1] In Perth, DPIRD similarly describes large, noisy roosts that disperse at dawn and foraging flocks travelling to feeding sites. [3]

Breeding is tied to tree cavities. The Australian Museum reports that eggs are laid on chewed, decayed wood, usually in a hollow eucalypt limb; both sexes prepare the cavity and feed young, while the female incubates. [1]

## Safer encounters with Rainbow Lorikeets

The safest and most useful encounter is an observational one. Give birds room to move between flowers, perches and hollows, keep noise low near a roost or nest cavity, and use binoculars or a camera rather than seeking close contact. This respects the species’ use of trees and helps avoid disrupting normal flocking, feeding and nesting behaviour. [1] [2]

If a Rainbow Lorikeet appears sick, injured or orphaned, do not turn the sighting into a do-it-yourself wildlife response. NSW Environment and Heritage warns that untrained wildlife responses can be dangerous and can harm people or animals. It says only licensed wildlife rehabilitation providers or registered veterinarians can take a sick, injured or orphaned native animal into care, and directs people to contact a local licensed wildlife rescue and rehabilitation group or a veterinarian for help. [4] Outside NSW, use the equivalent state or territory wildlife authority or licensed rescue service.

In Western Australia, an apparent Rainbow Lorikeet outside metropolitan Perth has extra significance: DPIRD asks people to report wild birds outside metropolitan Perth so the department can respond. [3] Follow the relevant authority’s current directions rather than assuming the rules from another state apply.

## If a person is bitten

Any animal can bite, including native animals. [5] If an animal bite breaks the skin, Healthdirect advises seeing a doctor as soon as possible; urgent emergency care is advised for a large or deep wound, a bite to the face or head, or bleeding that will not stop. [5] This is general wound guidance, not a claim that Rainbow Lorikeets carry a particular human disease. For urgent medical help, call triple zero (000); Healthdirect’s 24-hour helpline is 1800 022 222. [5]

## Frequently asked questions

### Are Rainbow Lorikeets native to Australia?

The species occurs naturally across coastal northern and eastern Australia. [1] The Perth population is different: the Australian Museum and DPIRD attribute it to aviary releases or escapees rather than treating it as part of the natural eastern and northern range. [1] [3]

### Where am I most likely to see one?

Look and listen in treed places with flowering shrubs or trees: rainforest and woodland, coastal vegetation, parks and well-treed urban areas are all documented habitat types. [1] [2]

### Why are Rainbow Lorikeets so loud at dusk?

The species is often seen in communal dusk roosts, and the Australian Museum describes frequent screeching and chattering. [1] Groups gathering near sunset can therefore be conspicuous and noisy. [1]

### How can I tell it from a Scaly-breasted Lorikeet?

Use several characteristics, not just a single colour. A Rainbow Lorikeet normally shows the blue or mauve head and belly and orange-yellow breast described above; the Australian Museum specifically identifies Scaly-breasted Lorikeets as a similar species. [1] If the view is brief or unclear, leave the sighting unidentified.

### What should I do if I see a bird that may need help?

Contact a local licensed wildlife rescue and rehabilitation group or a veterinarian for professional advice. NSW Environment and Heritage says untrained responders may cause harm and that only licensed rehabilitation providers or registered veterinarians can take sick, injured or orphaned native animals into care. [4]
