# Common Wombat (*Vombatus ursinus*)

The **common wombat** (*Vombatus ursinus*) is also called the **bare-nosed wombat**. It is a marsupial and the only living member of the genus *Vombatus*; the other living wombat species are hairy-nosed wombats in the genus *Lasiorhinus*. [1] This page concerns that one species—not “wombats” as a whole. In New South Wales, the bare-nosed wombat is the most common and widespread wombat species. [2]

WildGuardian lists this mammal with a **MEDIUM** risk label. The useful response is informed space: appreciate a wombat as a strong, wild burrowing animal, rather than treating it as an animal for close contact. Australian Museum notes that bare-nosed wombats can move faster than 40 km/h over short distances and records that people encounter them relatively infrequently because they are mainly nocturnal. [1]

## Identification: useful clues and their limits

A common wombat has a short, stocky, barrel-shaped body, a broad head, small eyes, a short strong neck, powerful shoulders and a very short tail hidden by fur. Its large, naked nose, coarse thick coat and short, slightly rounded ears distinguish it from the hairy-nosed wombats. [1] Victorian wildlife guidance describes the nose as shiny black, the ears as relatively small, triangular and slightly rounded, and the fur as varying from sandy through brown or black to grey. [3]

The bare nose is the best starting clue. By contrast, the southern and northern hairy-nosed wombats have finer hairs over the nose, longer and more pointed ears, and a longer, squarer muzzle. [3] Coat colour alone is a poor shortcut: the Australian Museum records considerable variation and notes that soil can stain the fur. [1]

Identification from a brief sighting therefore has limits. A broad, low animal with a pale, dark or dusty coat is not enough on its own. Look for the nose and ear shape from a respectful distance, and do not assume that body size settles the question. Size varies geographically: mainland animals average larger than Tasmanian and island animals, according to the Australian Museum. [1]

## Australian range and habitat

The common wombat’s present-day distribution is **discontinuous and fragmented**. Victorian government guidance places the species in Tasmania, eastern New South Wales and eastern Victoria, with scattered populations in south-eastern South Australia and south-western Victoria. [3] The Australian Museum likewise reports losses from parts of its former range, including much of western Victoria and many areas of New South Wales. [1] A broad distribution description should not be read as a guarantee that wombats occur throughout every district within those regions.

Habitat changes across that range. The Australian Museum identifies temperate forest-covered south-eastern Australia as the species’ main habitat. In southern Queensland and northern New South Wales, it occurs only in sclerophyll forest above 600 metres; in South Australia and Tasmania it can occur at lower elevations in woodland, coastal scrub and heathland. [1] In Victoria, the recorded habitat range includes sub-alpine heath and woodland, wet and dry forest, woodland, coastal scrub and wet heath. [3]

What those places need is more than vegetation. Common wombats depend on suitable burrow sites near food. [3] In New South Wales, wombat burrows are often on gully sides in well-drained, diggable soils; they can extend up to 30 metres and several metres deep. [2] Tasmania Parks and Wildlife Service describes the common wombat as the largest burrowing herbivorous mammal, a useful reminder that burrows are a core part of its ecology rather than incidental holes in the landscape. [4]

## Grazing, burrows and daily activity

Common wombats are primarily grazers. Their diet includes fibrous native grasses, sedges and rushes, with food choice changing with what is available; Victorian guidance also lists roots, tubers and pasture grasses. [1] [3] Their continuously growing front teeth cut plant material efficiently. [1]

They are mostly active at night. The Australian Museum says a wombat commonly leaves its burrow after sunset to graze and generally returns before sunrise, while Victorian guidance says nocturnal grazing is typical when temperatures are lower. [1] [3] That pattern is not absolute. On cool or overcast days, wombats may forage for longer and be seen during daylight; in cold periods they may also graze or bask in the sun during the day. [1] [3] A daytime sighting by itself is therefore not proof that an animal is injured or unwell.

Burrows provide shelter from heat and cold, and wombats may share them. [2] Their social lives are not simply communal, however. NSW and Victorian guidance both describe feeding areas as defended or territorial, with scent marking and vocal or aggressive responses to intrusion. [2] [3] These facts help explain why a wombat should be observed without crowding it or trying to create an interaction.

## Safer encounters in wombat country

The safest wildlife encounter is a distant, unhurried one. Give a wombat clear space and a chance to continue on its own route; avoid turning a sighting into close contact. This is especially important around burrows and feeding areas, which are central to the species’ shelter and foraging ecology. [2] [3]

On roads through known wombat habitat, stay alert, particularly around the cooler and darker periods when the species is commonly active. Vehicle collisions are identified by NSW as a threat to wombats. [2] If you see a wombat from a vehicle, prioritise legal, controlled driving over a sudden manoeuvre.

A wombat seen during the day is not automatically a problem, but visible hair loss, thickened or inflamed skin, or an animal that appears in poor condition can be consistent with sarcoptic mange. [2] [3] For concerns about an apparently sick, injured or orphaned wombat, use the relevant state wildlife authority or a licensed wildlife rehabilitation organisation; Victoria’s wildlife authority directs people concerned about a mange-affected wombat to those services. [3]

## If a wombat bites or scratches someone

A wombat encounter can cause injury, even though bites from native animals are less commonly discussed than dog or cat bites. Healthdirect states that any animal, including native animals, can bite and that infection can occur when an animal bite breaks the skin. [5]

If a wombat bite or scratch **breaks the skin**, seek medical advice as soon as possible. [5] Go to an emergency department for a large or deep wound, a bite to the face or head, or bleeding that will not stop; for urgent medical help, Healthdirect says to call Triple Zero (000). [5] A clinician can assess the wound, infection risk and tetanus status. [5] This is general Australian health guidance, not a diagnosis for an individual injury.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a common wombat the same as a bare-nosed wombat?

Yes. Bare-nosed wombat is an alternative name for *Vombatus ursinus*, which is also commonly called the common wombat. [1]

### Where do common wombats live in Australia?

They occur in Tasmania and parts of south-eastern mainland Australia, but their distribution is fragmented. Government and museum sources describe populations in eastern New South Wales and Victoria, with scattered populations in south-eastern South Australia and south-western Victoria; habitat and local occurrence vary within that broad picture. [1] [3]

### What is the most reliable visible feature?

The large, naked nose is the key feature behind the name “bare-nosed wombat.” Coarse fur and relatively short, rounded ears also help distinguish it from hairy-nosed wombats. [1] [3]

### Are common wombats only active at night?

They are mostly nocturnal, but may forage or bask during daylight in cool or overcast conditions. A daytime observation alone does not establish that a wombat is sick. [1] [3]

### What should happen after a wombat bite that breaks skin?

Get medical advice as soon as possible. Healthdirect advises emergency-department care for a large or deep wound, a bite to the face or head, or bleeding that cannot be stopped. [5]
