# Irukandji Jellyfish (*Carukia barnesi*)

The Irukandji Jellyfish in this profile is *Carukia barnesi*, a very small box jellyfish. The word “Irukandji” is also used more broadly for a syndrome and for several related jellyfish, so a common-name match does not establish a species. [1] [2] This page concerns *C. barnesi* and the CRITICAL WildGuardian risk label is a public-safety label, not a conservation status.

A suspected tropical jellyfish sting is not a situation for close identification. An Irukandji sting can seem minor at first, yet serious symptoms can develop later. Queensland health guidance says Irukandji syndrome may be fatal and advises assessment at the nearest hospital. [3] If anyone is stung in tropical waters, get out of the water, call Triple Zero (000) for urgent help and follow the directions of emergency services.

## Why it is difficult to see

The bell of *C. barnesi* is transparent and only about 1–2 centimetres across. It has four tentacles, one from each corner structure of the bell; tentacles can reach about one metre. [1] [2] Its small size and transparency mean a person in the water may not see it, particularly with glare, waves or suspended sand.

Those details are useful biology, not a field test. Other marine animals can be small, transparent or difficult to see, and a person should never enter the water or touch an animal to check for Irukandji. The Australian Museum also notes specialised features such as distinctive clusters of stinging cells, but those are not features a beachgoer can assess safely. [1]

## Australian range and habitat

The Australian Museum records *C. barnesi* from Western Australia to Queensland, with uncommon records farther south off Sydney and Melbourne. [1] Research on its early life history documents north-eastern Australian occurrence from Lizard Island to Fraser Island and describes the medusa as oceanic and planktonic. [2]

It can occur around reefs and islands and may sometimes reach beaches. In north Queensland, published records place its seasonal presence broadly between November and May, but timing varies with location and conditions. [2] A calendar cannot tell a swimmer whether a particular beach is safe. Follow current warnings, lifesaver advice, closures and local stinger information instead.

## Safer choices in tropical waters

The most useful protection is preventing contact. Check local conditions before swimming, follow beach signs and choose patrolled areas where lifesavers are present. Do not assume that clear water, a lack of visible jellyfish or a familiar swimming place rules out risk.

A full-body stinger suit can reduce exposed skin, but it is not a guarantee. Keep children close, stay within local safety advice and avoid handling any jellyfish in the water or on the sand. A beach safety decision should be based on current local guidance rather than a personal species guess.

## If someone is bitten, stung or hurt

> **CALL 000 FIRST.** Call 000 now.

- Stay with the person and follow the 000 operator’s instructions.
- Serious symptoms can start well after a sting that seemed minor. Get help even if it feels mild.

WildGuardian does not write first-aid instructions. For what to do, use the official guidance: [Healthdirect — Sea creature bites and stings](https://www.healthdirect.gov.au/sea-creature-stings); [Queensland Government — Dangerous marine life](https://www.qld.gov.au/emergency/safety/dangerous-marine).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is every tiny tropical jellyfish an Irukandji Jellyfish?

Call 000 now. WildGuardian does not write first-aid instructions; follow the official guidance linked in the section above. [4] [5]

### Where in Australia is *Carukia barnesi* found?

The Australian Museum records it from Western Australia to Queensland, with rare southern records. [1] North-eastern research records extend from Lizard Island to Fraser Island. [2]

### Can an Irukandji sting be serious if it does not hurt much at first?

Yes. Queensland health guidance says symptoms of Irukandji syndrome can develop after the sting and that the condition may be fatal. [3] Seek urgent medical assessment rather than waiting for symptoms to settle or trying to identify the animal.

### Does CRITICAL mean the species is critically endangered?

No. It is WildGuardian’s risk label for the potential severity of a human sting. It does not state the species’ conservation classification.
