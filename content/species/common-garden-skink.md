# Common Garden Skink (*Lampropholis guichenoti*)

The **Common Garden Skink** is a small native Australian skink commonly noticed in gardens, parks and bushland in the south-east of the continent. Its accepted scientific name is *Lampropholis guichenoti* (Duméril & Bibron, 1839), a member of the skink family Scincidae. Authoritative Australian records also use the names **Garden Skink** and **Pale-flecked Garden Sunskink**. [1] [2]

WildGuardian’s supplied **LOW** risk label is about routine human encounters, not a conservation assessment. Separately, the Queensland Government’s WildNet record reports this species as Least Concern under its NCA status field and “not listed” under its EPBC status field. [3] A small lizard in a sunny garden is therefore usually best treated as wildlife to observe without interference, rather than as a pest or a pet.

## Identification limits: useful field clues, not proof

A Common Garden Skink is generally grey-brown or olive-brown above, often with a darker line along the middle of the back and a darker side stripe. It can have a flecked appearance, a whitish to silvery-grey underside and a coppery-toned head. [2] The South Australian government’s Green Adelaide guide similarly describes a small brown or grey skink with a coppery, flecked head. [4]

Size is useful only as broad context. Museums Victoria records a snout-to-vent length of up to 6 cm and a maximum total length of 10 cm, while Green Adelaide gives an overall length of 8–10 cm. [2] [4] A tail makes up a substantial part of a skink’s total length, so measurements taken in different ways need not match exactly.

These markings are not enough to confirm an identification in every setting. The Australian National University’s Lucid key notes that another small local skink closely resembles the Garden Skink, and that colour and markings are used to distinguish them. [5] Distance, shade, wet skin, a missing or regrowing tail, and a fleeting view through vegetation can all make those features harder to assess. For a practical encounter, it is better to regard “small striped garden skink” as a provisional observation than to close the gap for certainty.

## Australian range and habitat

The documented Australian range runs from south-eastern Queensland through eastern New South Wales and southern Victoria into south-eastern South Australia. [2] [5] The Lucid key also records the species across much of the ACT, from Brindabella forests to suburban Canberra, and reports no recorded Tasmanian populations. [5] These statements describe the broad range, not a guarantee that the skink occurs in every garden or reserve within it.

The species uses varied south-eastern habitats. Museums Victoria lists wet and dry sclerophyll forest, coastal and mountain heath, and suburban gardens. [2] The ANU Lucid profile adds woodlands and grasslands, and describes the skink as adaptable to a variety of habitat situations. [5] In Adelaide, the state government identifies the Common Garden Skink as one of the small skinks people are especially likely to encounter in backyards, parks and reserves while it seeks a sunny place to bask. [4]

At a smaller scale, ground-level structure matters. The skink is reported for leaf litter, grass and rocky areas, where it forages and basks; Museums Victoria likewise notes leaf litter and rock piles. [2] [5] That helps explain why a sighting may be brief: an animal can move quickly between cover, a warm surface and food-bearing ground rather than staying exposed.

## Ecology and behaviour

The Common Garden Skink is diurnal, meaning it is active during the day, and it is mostly ground-dwelling. It may be seen basking on rocks or moving through leaf litter while foraging. [2] [5] Its food is small invertebrates; the sources specifically mention ants and other invertebrates. [2] [5] In this role, it is part of the food web both as a small predator of invertebrates and as prey for other animals.

Season affects visibility. The ANU source reports that in colder parts of the range, including higher ACT areas, the species becomes inactive in winter, whereas in warmer areas it can remain active year-round. [5] That is a useful explanation for a change in sightings across seasons, not a timetable that can predict when an individual will be present.

Reproduction includes communal egg-laying. Museums Victoria reports that females gather to lay eggs in communal nests, which may contain more than 200 eggs, with up to four eggs from each female. [2] The Australian Government’s *Fauna of Australia* account specifically identifies *L. guichenoti* as an example of communal nesting among Australian skinks. [6] Observing a small skink near litter, rocks or soil does not reveal whether it is associated with breeding, so those places should not be disturbed for a closer look.

When threatened by a predator, the Common Garden Skink can drop its tail; Green Adelaide notes that the detached tail may continue to move while the skink escapes, and that regrowth takes considerable energy over months. [4] This defensive ability is not a sign that contact is harmless. It is another reason a calm, hands-off encounter is better for the animal.

## Safer encounters

For a LOW-risk skink, the most practical safety measure is space. Watch from where you are, keep the encounter quiet and allow the animal an unobstructed route back into its normal cover. That approach fits its documented ground-level foraging, basking and use of leaf litter, grass and rocks. [2] [5]

A sighting beside a path, patio, garden bed or park rock does not require a person to intervene. Stepping back with children or companion animals instead of crowding the skink reduces pressure on a small animal whose normal response is to seek cover. Domestic cats are identified as a local urban threat in the ANU profile. [5] A photograph, if taken, should be from a respectful distance and should not alter the animal’s behaviour.

This page intentionally offers no capture, handling, feeding, relocation, pest-control, nest-removal or wildlife-treatment directions. The useful outcome is simple coexistence: leave the skink’s routine undisturbed and enjoy the short observation.

## Medical response where relevant

A Common Garden Skink encounter is not presented here as a venom emergency. If any native animal interaction breaks a person’s skin, however, use ordinary Australian health advice for an animal wound rather than making assumptions from the animal’s small size. Healthdirect states that native animals can bite and advises seeing a doctor as soon as possible if an animal bite has broken the skin. [7]

Go to an emergency department if the wound is large or deep, involves the face or head, or bleeding cannot be stopped; Healthdirect says not to drive yourself and to call Triple Zero (000) for an ambulance when needed. [7] This is general wound escalation advice, not a claim of a special toxin or species-specific medical effect for *L. guichenoti*.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the Common Garden Skink’s scientific name *Lampropholis guichenoti*?

Yes. The Atlas of Living Australia lists *Lampropholis guichenoti* (Duméril & Bibron, 1839) as the accepted name and places it in Scincidae. [1]

### Is a Common Garden Skink the same as a Pale-flecked Garden Sunskink?

Yes. Museums Victoria uses **Pale-flecked Garden Sunskink**, and the Atlas of Living Australia records that name as an Australian vernacular name for *L. guichenoti*. [1] [2]

### Where might I see one in Australia?

Look for the species in suitable habitat across the south-east, from south-eastern Queensland through eastern New South Wales and southern Victoria to south-eastern South Australia. It also occurs through much of the ACT, including suburban Canberra. [2] [5]

### Are Common Garden Skinks active at night?

No. The consulted Australian sources describe the species as diurnal, with basking and foraging during the day. [2] [5]

### What should I do if I find a small skink in my garden?

Treat it as a brief wildlife observation. Give it space, avoid interfering with its cover or behaviour, and keep the setting calm so it can continue on its own. Its known use of leaf litter, rocks and other ground cover means it may disappear quickly from view. [2] [5]
