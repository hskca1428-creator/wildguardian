# Eastern Water Dragon (*Intellagama lesueurii*)

The Eastern Water Dragon is a large, conspicuous Australian agamid lizard often seen beside creeks, rivers, ponds and other waterways. WildGuardian’s risk label for this record is **LOW**. That does not make close contact a good idea: a large adult has sharp claws and can deliver a serious bite if approached. [2] [5]

There is an important name detail. This record uses the species name *Intellagama lesueurii*, while the Eastern Water Dragon is the accepted subspecies *Intellagama lesueurii lesueurii*. The Atlas of Living Australia, using the Australian Faunal Directory as its name authority, lists it in the family Agamidae. [1] The name *Physignathus lesueurii lesueurii* appears in older material because the Australian water dragon was moved to the genus *Intellagama* after taxonomic review. [2]

## Identification and its limits

Look for a robust lizard with a long tail, strong limbs, a deep angular head and a conspicuous crest of spiny scales from the nape along the back and tail. For the Eastern subspecies, the most useful field feature is a dark stripe running from the eye over the ear and down the neck. Its upper surface is grey to brownish-grey, with darker striping; mature males can have a bright red chest and upper belly. [2] NSW National Parks similarly describes an olive-to-brown lizard with a dark stripe behind the eye, reddish throat tones and a row of spines from head down the back. [3]

Those clues narrow an identification but do not replace careful observation. The water dragon species has two recognised Australian subspecies. The Gippsland Water Dragon, *I. l. howittii*, replaces the Eastern Water Dragon in the south and differs in colour pattern, including the absence of the dark eye-to-ear stripe. [2] A large, spiny lizard beside water is therefore not enough on its own to establish the subspecies, especially near the changeover in southern New South Wales. A photo may support a likely identification, but it cannot safely justify approaching the animal.

## Australian range and habitat

The species occurs in eastern Australia and southern New Guinea, but this page is about the Australian Eastern subspecies. *I. l. lesueurii* occurs down Australia’s east coast from Cooktown in Queensland to the New South Wales south coast around Kangaroo Valley; farther south, the Gippsland subspecies occurs into eastern Victoria. [2] NSW National Parks describes the Eastern Water Dragon as a subaquatic lizard of healthy waterways along eastern NSW, from Nowra north to about halfway up Cape York Peninsula. [3]

It is strongly associated with riparian habitat: the land and vegetation alongside waterways. Flowing water, tree cover and places to bask are important features. The Australian Museum reports that the species uses habitats from tropical rainforest in the north to alpine streams in the south, and can persist in built-up areas where suitable water, cover, basking sites and fair water quality are present. [2] In local settings it may turn up around creeks, watercourses, coastal areas and backyard ponds, which is why people can encounter it in parks and suburban green space. [5]

A sighting beside water is useful context, not a reason to assume an animal needs help. Water dragons commonly use rocks, branches and banks for basking, and they are powerful swimmers. [3]

## Ecology and behaviour

Eastern Water Dragons are diurnal, semi-aquatic omnivores. Young water dragons are insectivorous, while older animals eat a broader mix that can include insects and other invertebrates, molluscs, crustaceans, fruit and flowers. [2] NSW National Parks also records insects, frogs, yabbies, fruit and berries in their diet. [3] This varied feeding makes the lizard part of freshwater-edge food webs rather than simply a visitor to them.

They are alert animals with an effective escape response. A water dragon disturbed near a creek may drop or run toward water and dive in; the Australian Museum notes that it can remain submerged for around an hour. [2] Seeing one disappear from a rock with a splash is therefore normal behaviour, not necessarily a sign of injury.

Activity changes with weather and latitude. In the Sydney region, water dragons are usually active from September to June and become inactive in the cooler months; the Museum describes this winter state as brumation. [2] That regional timing should not be treated as a calendar for every population across Queensland, New South Wales and Victoria, where local temperatures differ.

Where habitat is suitable, several animals may occur together. Groups can include females and young with a dominant male, and water dragons use visual displays such as head-bobbing and postures in social interactions. [2] From a visitor’s perspective, the useful takeaway is simple: apparent tolerance of people does not mean the animal is tame or wants contact.

## Safer encounters

The best encounter is a quiet, distant one. Watch from where you are, give the lizard room and let it choose its own route to water, cover or a basking place. Do not approach or attempt to feed a water dragon. Northern Beaches Council gives that advice specifically because they have sharp claws and can bite; the Australian National Botanic Gardens also asks visitors not to approach, touch or feed water dragons. [5] [4]

If a dragon changes posture, moves away, runs for cover or enters water, stop advancing and increase the distance. Do not follow it into vegetation, onto rocks or to the water’s edge. Keeping to paths and observing rather than interacting protects both people and the animal. In places where companion animals are permitted, follow the local rules and keep them under effective control so wildlife has space to retreat. The NSW Department of Education notes that supervised dogs and cats near bushland can injure native reptiles. [6]

Do not leave food scraps near waterways or treat a water dragon as a park attraction. Its normal diet is varied and its welfare is better served by intact habitat and an undisturbed opportunity to forage. [2] If you are unsure whether a lizard is an Eastern Water Dragon, use the same distance-first approach rather than trying to obtain a closer view.

## If a person is bitten or scratched

Bites and scratches can break skin. For any animal bite or scratch, Healthdirect advises gently washing the wound with soap and running water for about five minutes, patting it dry with a clean cloth and covering it with a clean dressing. Apply pressure if it is bleeding heavily. [7]

Seek medical help quickly if a wound is deep, shows signs of infection, is on the hand, face, feet or genitals, or if skin has been broken; Healthdirect notes that a clinician may need to assess tetanus protection or antibiotics. [7] Call Triple Zero (000) for an ambulance if a bite wound is on the face or head, is large or deep, or bleeding cannot be stopped. [7] People who are older, immunocompromised, pregnant, or caring for young children should take particular care with animal contact because these groups can be at higher risk of illness from germs animals carry. [7]

## Frequently asked questions

### Is an Eastern Water Dragon dangerous?

WildGuardian classifies this record as LOW risk, but it is still a wild lizard. Large adults have sharp claws and may bite if approached, so enjoy them from a distance rather than trying to touch or feed them. [2] [5]

### Where are Eastern Water Dragons found in Australia?

The Eastern subspecies occurs along the east coast from Cooktown in Queensland to around Kangaroo Valley on the NSW south coast. The species also has a Gippsland subspecies farther south into eastern Victoria. [2]

### How can I distinguish it from a Gippsland Water Dragon?

The Eastern Water Dragon typically has a dark stripe from the eye over the ear and down the neck. The Gippsland Water Dragon lacks that stripe, but range and a clear view of the animal still matter for a confident identification. [2]

### Why do they dive into water?

Water dragons are strong swimmers and commonly escape disturbance by diving from a basking place into water. They can stay submerged for around an hour, according to the Australian Museum. [2]

### Can Eastern Water Dragons live in cities?

Yes. The species can occur in built-up areas where there is suitable water, tree cover, basking space and fair water quality. That does not mean it is a domestic animal or should be fed. [2]
