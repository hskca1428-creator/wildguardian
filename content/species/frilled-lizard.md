# Frilled Lizard (*Chlamydosaurus kingii*) in Australia

The Frilled Lizard, *Chlamydosaurus kingii*, is an Australian dragon lizard in the family Agamidae. “Frilled Lizard” is the preferred common name in the Museums Victoria collection, while “frill-neck lizard” and “frillneck” are also widely used in Australian sources. [1] [2] WildGuardian’s **LOW** label is a practical profile risk label; it is not a government conservation listing or a substitute for local wildlife advice.

A frilled lizard is memorable because of the broad fold of skin around its throat. Most of the time that frill is folded against the neck. When the animal feels threatened, it may open the frill, gape and hiss as part of a defensive display. [2] That display is an excellent reason to step back: the aim is to reduce pressure on the lizard, not to get a closer photograph.

## Identification: useful signs and their limits

The folded or expanded neck frill is the strongest visual clue, but it should not be treated as the only test from a distant or poor-quality sighting. Frilled lizards are mottled and rough-textured, which helps them blend with bark and woodland surroundings. They may keep the trunk between themselves and an observer, and can run on their hind legs to a nearby tree when disturbed. [3] A lizard that disappears around a trunk can therefore be difficult to confirm from one brief view.

Look for a reasonably large, long-tailed dragon lizard associated with a tree trunk, stump or lower branches, and for the distinctive expandable frill. The frill does not need to be visible in a calm animal. The Australian Museum notes that bearded dragons can be confused with frilled lizards; bearded dragons have a much smaller, spiny beard rather than the extensive neck frill of *C. kingii*. [2] Colour alone is unreliable because it varies geographically. [2]

This page can support a likely identification, not replace expert confirmation. Do not approach, touch or try to make an animal display for identification. A clear observation from a respectful distance is more useful than a close encounter that changes its behaviour.

## Australian range and habitat

In Australia, the species’ documented range extends from the Kimberley region of Western Australia across the Northern Territory’s Top End to Cape York Peninsula and coastal eastern Queensland. It also occurs in southern New Guinea. [2] It is a northern and eastern Australian species, not a Central Australian one. [4]

Within that range, frilled lizards use open scrub, woodland and forest. [2] They use the ground as well as lower trunks, branches and dead stumps, and may sleep on elevated perches. [2] The University of New England Natural History Museum describes them as inhabitants of tropical woodlands in northern and eastern Australia. [3] A suitable-looking tree in the right broad region is therefore a helpful clue, but it is not enough by itself to establish that a lizard is a frilled lizard.

For visitors and residents, this means sightings are most plausible in tropical or seasonally tropical woodland settings in the north, including places where trunks and standing timber give the animal cover and a lookout point. It does **not** mean the lizard is present in every park, suburb or patch of woodland across that broad range.

## Ecology and behaviour

Frilled lizards are active by day. They forage on the ground and in vegetation, while also using trunks as lookout points. The Australian Museum described a diet of live spiders, insects and occasional small vertebrates; the UNE museum notes hunting from a perch on a standing trunk, around one to two metres above the ground, to ambush passing prey. [2] [3]

Their response to disturbance is primarily avoidance and camouflage. A lizard may freeze against a trunk with its frill down, shift out of sight around the tree, or run to cover. [2] [3] If escape is not immediate, the familiar frill, open mouth and hissing can make the animal look much more imposing. That is a defensive response, not a cue to keep following it. [2]

Season changes matter in northern Australia. A peer-reviewed study of the wet–dry tropics found frillneck lizards conspicuous during the wet season but rarely seen in the dry season. In the dry season, food volume was lower and energy expenditure fell substantially; the study found that the lizards still fed rather than becoming wholly inactive. [5] The older Northern Territory Parks and Wildlife education resource similarly reports that frilled lizards are seen much more often in the wet season, when they are harder to spot in rough-barked trees during the dry. [4]

Reproductive timing should be interpreted locally. In a Kakadu National Park population studied from 1992 to 1994, reproduction occurred from November to April and coincided with monsoonal conditions. [6] That is evidence from one northern Australian population, not a calendar that should be assumed for every frilled lizard throughout Australia.

## Safer encounters with frilled lizards

The safest encounter is a brief, quiet one that lets the animal choose its own cover. Pause well back, keep children and companion animals away, and avoid crowding a trunk, stump or potential escape route. Do not touch, feed, chase, corner or attempt to move the lizard. The NT Government says lizards commonly enter care after domestic-animal attacks and warns that inappropriate care by inexperienced people is a common problem. [7]

If a frilled lizard is displaying its frill, increase the distance rather than waiting for a better image. Do not use objects, noise or close movement to provoke a display. If you are taking a photograph, use distance and a long lens; do not put yourself or the lizard in a stressful position for a closer shot.

If the animal appears injured, sick or unable to leave danger, do not try home treatment or take it into care. In the Northern Territory, official guidance is to contact a local wildlife care organisation, Wildlife Operations or a veterinary clinic; native wildlife care requires a wildlife carer’s permit. [8] Elsewhere in Australia, contact the relevant state or territory wildlife authority, a local licensed wildlife rescue service or a veterinarian for direction. This keeps decisions about care with appropriately trained and authorised people.

## If a bite or scratch breaks the skin

A frilled lizard may bite if it is cornered, so prevention is the priority. [2] The following is general Australian health guidance for any animal bite or scratch, not a species-specific envenomation protocol. Healthdirect advises gently washing the wound with soap and running water for about five minutes, patting it dry and covering it with a clean dressing. If the skin is broken, see a doctor promptly; urgent medical help is also needed for a deep wound, signs of infection, or a wound on the hand, face, feet or genitals. [9]

Call Triple Zero (000) for an ambulance if an animal-bite wound is on the face or head, is large or deep, or bleeding cannot be stopped. [9] Healthdirect also advises that people with weakened immune systems avoid reptiles because they can carry harmful germs such as *Salmonella*. [9] Avoiding contact and washing hands after any unplanned animal contact are sensible safeguards.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why does a frilled lizard open its frill?

It is a defensive display. The frill is usually folded at the neck, but a threatened lizard can spread it while gaping and hissing to look larger and more formidable. [2] Give the animal space rather than trying to trigger or prolong the display.

### Where in Australia might I see one?

The documented Australian range runs from the Kimberley across the Top End to Cape York Peninsula and coastal eastern Queensland. [2] It is associated with open scrub, woodland and forest, especially in northern and eastern Australia. [2] [3]

### Are frilled lizards easier to see at some times of year?

In the northern wet–dry tropics, studies and NT Parks and Wildlife material report more sightings in the wet season and much lower conspicuousness in the dry season. [4] [5] A dry-season sighting is still possible, so absence from view does not mean absence from the area.

### What should I do if I find an injured frilled lizard?

Keep your distance and seek advice from the responsible local wildlife authority, wildlife rescue organisation or veterinarian. In the Northern Territory, the government directs people to a local wildlife care organisation, Wildlife Operations or a veterinary clinic, and requires a permit for wildlife care. [8] Do not attempt to treat or keep the animal yourself.

### Should I seek medical help after a frilled-lizard bite or scratch?

If skin is broken, Healthdirect advises seeing a doctor promptly. Wash the wound gently with soap and running water for about five minutes, dry it and cover it with a clean dressing; call 000 for severe bleeding, or a large or deep wound on the face or head. [9]
