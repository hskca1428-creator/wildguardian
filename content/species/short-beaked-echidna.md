# Short-beaked echidna (*Tachyglossus aculeatus*)

The **short-beaked echidna** is a native Australian monotreme: a mammal that lays eggs. [2] [3] Its accepted scientific name is *Tachyglossus aculeatus*, in the family Tachyglossidae and order Monotremata. [1] It is the only echidna species found in Australia; the long-beaked echidna is a New Guinea species. [2]

A quiet animal with a spiny back and pointed snout can be an exciting sight, whether it is in bushland, on an island reserve or close to a suburb. The best encounter is a low-impact one: recognise what you are seeing, allow the animal room to continue, and seek qualified local help only if there is a genuine welfare or safety concern. The **LOW** label attached to this WildGuardian record is a database risk label, not a substitute for a legal conservation listing or a local assessment.

## Identification: what you can and cannot confirm

A short-beaked echidna has sharp spines over a short, stocky body, short legs and a long snout. Australian Museum gives a size range of 40–55 centimetres; NSW Environment gives 35–53 centimetres for adults. [2] [3] Variation is normal. NSW Environment notes that northern animals may be light brown, while animals farther south can be darker and have thicker hair; Tasmanian animals are described as black. [3]

Those broad features make an echidna distinctive, but they do not establish an animal’s sex, age, health, local population or subspecies. Nor should colour alone be used to diagnose illness or identify a location. The Atlas of Living Australia recognises *Tachyglossus aculeatus* as an accepted species and lists several subspecies, which is a useful reminder that a species-level observation should remain at species level unless an expert has assessed it. [1]

If an animal has spines, a long narrow snout and the compact outline described above, **short-beaked echidna** is the appropriate Australian species name. Do not confuse that identification with a reason to approach. Its documented retreat behaviour is a reason to observe from a respectful distance rather than test an identification at close range. [3]

## Australian range and habitat

The short-beaked echidna occurs throughout mainland Australia and Tasmania. [2] NSW Environment also records it on King, Flinders and Kangaroo islands. [3] This broad Australian distribution does not mean every patch of country is suitable or that an animal will be easy to find. In NSW, the species is described as quiet and reclusive, so people may live near echidnas without often seeing one. [3]

Its habitats are notably varied. The Australian Museum lists forests and woodlands, heath, grasslands and arid environments. [2] NSW Environment describes echidnas across almost all habitat types, from snow-covered mountains to deserts, and notes that they can occur in urban areas. [3] On Kangaroo Island, South Australia’s National Parks and Wildlife Service reports short-beaked echidnas around hollow logs, under debris, in self-constructed burrows and among tree roots. [4]

Habitat is therefore more than a vegetation label. A sighting at a track or garden edge does not, on its own, show how many echidnas use an area, whether they are resident, or whether the wider population is secure.

## Behaviour and ecology

For much of the year, echidnas are solitary. NSW Environment says that individual territories are large and can overlap, and that animals probably use their fine sense of smell to find one another in the breeding season. [3] South Australian park staff likewise describe Kangaroo Island echidnas as solitary. [4] A lone animal is therefore expected; it does not, by itself, indicate abandonment or distress.

Echidnas mainly forage for ants and termites. They use a pointed snout and claws to open nests, then catch prey with a long sticky tongue. [2] NSW Environment says termites are preferred and explains that, because echidnas have no teeth, food is ground between the tongue and the floor of the mouth. [3] Diggings, paused attention at a nest or a slow stop-start search can be ordinary foraging behaviour rather than a sign that the animal needs human intervention.

Activity can change with conditions and place. In warm areas, NSW Environment reports feeding during cooler morning and evening periods and resting through the day’s heat; in southern Australia, it reports that echidnas may stop eating in colder months before feeding heavily in spring. [3] On Kangaroo Island, echidnas may feed by day and night. [4] These observations are useful context, not a timetable for predicting any individual animal.

Like the platypus, the short-beaked echidna is an egg-laying mammal. It lays one egg at a time, according to the Australian Museum. [2] This unusual reproductive biology is one reason an echidna is sometimes called a monotreme, but it is still a mammal—not a reptile.

## Safer encounters: give an echidna space

Echidnas are shy. When frightened, an animal may curl into a ball with its snout and legs tucked beneath it, wedge itself under rocks, or burrow into soft soil. [3] That defensive response is a clear reason not to crowd the animal. Watch quietly from a distance and allow an unobstructed route away.

If an echidna appears sick or injured, has been involved in a collision, is entangled, or is in an immediately unsafe setting, put **your own safety first** and contact the wildlife authority or local wildlife rescue service for the state or territory where you are. Victoria’s official echidna service directs people to local wildlife carers and rescue organisations and specifically includes injury, animal attack, fire impact, heat stress, entanglement and traffic collision among its report categories. [5] In South Australia, National Parks and Wildlife Service directs people who find an injured native animal outside a park to a local wildlife rescue group. [6]

Do not assume that a curled-up echidna needs assistance. That is a documented protective behaviour. [3] Equally, do not rely on this page to decide whether an injury is minor. A local authority or authorised service can assess the circumstances under the rules that apply where the animal is found.

Road awareness matters. The Australian Museum identifies vehicles among the hazards that can kill short-beaked echidnas. [2] In Flinders Chase National Park, the local parks service advises drivers to be particularly vigilant for wildlife at dawn and dusk, to slow down and to be prepared to stop. [4] Follow local road conditions and park directions rather than putting yourself or other road users at risk.

### If a person is hurt or worried after contact

This page does not provide diagnosis or treatment. If someone is injured or has a health concern after any wildlife encounter, seek professional health advice. Healthdirect Australia says to call **000** for urgent medical help; for non-urgent health advice, its 24-hour helpline is **1800 022 222**. [7]

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a short-beaked echidna a mammal?

Yes. *Tachyglossus aculeatus* is classified in Mammalia and Monotremata. [1] It is a monotreme, which means it is a mammal that lays eggs. [2]

### Where in Australia do short-beaked echidnas live?

They occur throughout mainland Australia and Tasmania, as well as King, Flinders and Kangaroo islands. [2] [3] They use habitats ranging from forest, woodland and heath to grassland and arid environments. [2]

### Why has an echidna curled into a spiny ball?

Curling up is one of its protective responses when frightened; it may also wedge itself under rocks or burrow into soft soil. [3] Give it room and do not interpret the response as an invitation for close contact.

### Do echidnas only come out at night?

No single schedule applies everywhere. NSW Environment describes cooler-morning and evening feeding in warm areas, while South Australian park staff report day and night feeding on Kangaroo Island. [3] [4]

### Are short-beaked echidnas protected?

Wildlife law is state- and territory-specific. NSW Environment says native mammals, except dingoes, are protected in NSW under the *Biodiversity Conservation Act 2016*. [3] Victoria’s wildlife service says it is illegal there to take wildlife from the wild, including carcasses or samples. [5] Check the relevant jurisdiction’s current guidance for the place of an encounter.
