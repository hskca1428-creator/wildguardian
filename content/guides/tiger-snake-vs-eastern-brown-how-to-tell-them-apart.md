# Tiger snake vs eastern brown: how to tell them apart

> **Emergency:** A suspected snake bite is a medical emergency. Call **000** immediately and ask for an ambulance; do not wait to identify the snake or for symptoms to develop. Keep the person still and follow official first-aid advice. [3] [5]

Tiger snakes (*Notechis scutatus*) and eastern brown snakes (*Pseudonaja textilis*) are both venomous Australian elapids. A sighting deserves calm, cautious behaviour, not a close inspection. Queensland’s environment department notes that individuals within one snake species can vary greatly, and that some snakes are difficult to identify positively; its direction is never to approach a snake or assume it is non-venomous. [1] [2] [4]

The safest answer to “tiger snake or eastern brown?” is often **“unknown snake—keep clear.”** Colour, bands, size, head shape, a defensive posture and habitat can contribute to a description, but none gives a member of the public a safe reason to get closer, touch the animal or try to move it. Tiger snakes can be banded or plain, while eastern brown snakes can be very dark, light tan or banded when young. The Australian Museum specifically notes that a large, banded tiger snake can be confused with a banded eastern brown. [1] [2]

## What to do when you see an unknown snake

Stop and create space. Snakes commonly retreat when encountered, but can become defensive if they feel threatened. Back away to a safe distance and allow it to move away. Do not try to catch, kill, prod, corner or otherwise interfere with it. [6]

A practical response is to keep other people and pets out of the area, watch only from well back if necessary to prevent someone walking into the snake, and seek appropriate local wildlife assistance if the animal cannot be safely left alone. Do not make an identification attempt the priority. Most bites occur when people try to capture or kill snakes, according to Queensland Government advice. [6]

If a bite may have occurred, switch immediately from wildlife observation to emergency response: **call 000 first**. A bite may be painless and may leave no obvious marks, so a person who believes they were bitten needs urgent medical assessment even if they initially feel well. [3] [5]

## The most useful visual clues—and their limits

Think of the following as details to note **from a safe distance**, not a field test. They may help describe a snake to an authorised responder or clinician, but they do not safely prove which species it is.

### Tiger snake: often robust, sometimes banded, never reliably “tiger-striped”

Tiger snake is the common name for the highly variable *Notechis scutatus*. The Australian Museum describes the snake as having a robust, muscular body and a moderately wide, deep head that is only slightly distinct from the neck. Its common name comes from yellow-and-black cross-bands found in some populations, but the species does not always show that pattern. [2]

A commonly seen form is dark olive-brown to blackish-brown with off-white to yellowish cross-bands of variable thickness. Yet entirely patternless snakes occur even in populations where bands are common, and unbanded animals can range from yellowish-brown to black. That variation means a plain dark snake is not automatically an eastern brown, and visible bands are not enough to call a snake a tiger. [2]

A tiger snake may look particularly plausible near water. The species is often associated with creeks, dams, drains, lagoons, wetlands and swamps, and can also occur in degraded grazing land where water and shelter are available. Habitat is a clue only: it is not an identification tool, and the places people describe as “wet” or “dry” are not fixed boundaries for either species. [2]

### Eastern brown: often slender-looking, but much more than brown

The eastern brown snake has a slender to moderate build, with a smallish head that is barely distinct from the neck. The Australian Museum describes its upper body colour as almost any shade of brown, from near black through light tan, chestnut and burnt orange; it is usually fairly uniform above. [1]

Young eastern browns make colour-based identification even less dependable. Hatchlings have a prominent dark patch across the head and nape, and some have dark bands along the body. The markings generally fade as they mature, but in some populations bands remain into adulthood. A banded young or adult snake must not be dismissed as “only a tiger snake” or treated as harmless. [1]

Eastern browns use many habitat types, although they favour open landscapes such as woodland, scrubland and savannah grassland. They are also found around rural areas modified for agriculture and on the suburban edge of many towns and cities. This broad habitat range is another reason that an eastern brown cannot be ruled out merely because a snake is near a shed, paddock, grassland or a built-up fringe. [1]

## Distribution and season do not settle the question

Tiger snakes have a fragmented distribution across two broad areas: south-eastern Australia, including Bass Strait islands and Tasmania, and south-western Australia. Eastern brown snakes are widespread in eastern Australia from northern Queensland to South Australia, with isolated populations in central and western Northern Territory. Their ranges therefore overlap in parts of southern and eastern Australia; state or region alone is not a safe diagnosis. [1] [2]

In south-east Queensland, official guidance illustrates how local knowledge can refine—not replace—caution. It records eastern tiger snakes in wetter coastal heath and moist rainforest areas outside Brisbane, while eastern brown snakes occur in a range of habitats and are the only one of those two regularly found in Brisbane’s suburbs. That does not turn a Brisbane or bushland sighting into a positive identification. [4]

Warm conditions also do not separate the species. Tiger snakes are typically more active in warmer months but may bask on warm winter days. Eastern brown snakes can be active on mild winter days, and are primarily daytime hunters, though very hot weather can shift their activity later in the day. Treat a snake sighting with the same care in every season. [1] [2]

## Why visual overlap matters more than a checklist

There is a genuine temptation to use a single feature: “thick body means tiger,” “brown body means eastern brown,” or “near water means tiger.” These shortcuts fail because natural variation is large. The Australian Museum notes that patternless tiger snakes can resemble several other venomous snakes, while eastern browns can be confused with other *Pseudonaja* species and generally require close inspection to separate. Close inspection is exactly what a bystander should not attempt. [1] [2]

A defensive display is not a safe test either. Eastern browns may raise the front of the body and spread the neck if cornered or provoked, and tiger snakes also defend themselves when threatened. The safe action is to avoid causing either response: do not approach, block the snake’s path, or try to make it move for a better look. [1] [2] [6]

## A safe way to describe a sighting

If you were already at a safe distance, make a mental note rather than moving closer for details. Useful, non-contact observations include:

- the suburb or general locality, and whether the place was near water, grassland, bushland, a paddock or a building;
- whether the body appeared mostly plain or showed cross-bands, and its broad colour in light;
- whether the snake appeared slender or more robust; and
- where it went, so that others can avoid the area.

Do not chase it for a photograph. ANZCOR says a digital photo may help identification **only if it is safe to take**, and strongly recommends against attempts to kill the snake because of the risk of bites. [5]

## If a bite is possible: act before identification

Treat every suspected Australian snake bite as potentially life-threatening. Call **000** for an ambulance, keep the person still, reassured and under constant observation, and follow the dispatcher’s instructions. ANZCOR recommends pressure bandaging with immobilisation and immediate CPR if the person is unresponsive and not breathing normally. [3] [5]

Do **not** wash the bite site, cut or suck the wound, or apply an arterial tourniquet. Do not try to catch or kill the snake for identification. Healthdirect and ANZCOR specifically advise against these actions. [3] [5]

## FAQs

### Are tiger snakes always banded?

No. Although some tiger snakes have prominent pale or yellowish cross-bands, entirely patternless animals occur and may be yellowish-brown through to black. [2]

### Are eastern brown snakes always brown?

No. Eastern browns can be near black, light tan, chestnut or burnt orange. Young snakes can have dark head-and-neck markings or body bands, and some adults retain bands. [1]

### Can habitat tell me which snake it is?

No. Tiger snakes are often associated with wet places, while eastern browns favour open landscapes and occur across a broad range of environments. Both the setting and the snake’s colour are supporting clues at most, not a safe identification. [1] [2]

### What if I cannot tell which species it is?

Treat it as a potentially dangerous snake: keep your distance, do not handle or provoke it, and allow it space to retreat. If there is any possibility of a bite, call **000** immediately rather than waiting for a species identification. [3] [5] [6]
